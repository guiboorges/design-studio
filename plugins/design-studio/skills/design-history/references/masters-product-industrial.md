## Raymond Loewy (1893–1986) — the showman who made industrial design a business

**Base / discipline / era:** Born in Paris on 5 November 1893. He arrived in New York in 1919 and died in Monte Carlo on 14 July 1986 [1]. He was a consultant industrial designer covering products, transportation, packaging, and corporate identity, and worked from about 1929 to 1980 [1].

**Key works**

| Work | Year | Why it matters |
|------|------|----------------|
| Gestetner duplicator restyling | 1929 | His first industrial-design commission: a new shell over existing machinery [1]. |
| Coldspot refrigerator (Sears) | 1930s | The product that established his reputation [1]. |
| Pennsylvania Railroad S1 locomotive (streamlined shell) | Completed 1939 | Its streamlined Art Deco shell was tested as a clay model in a wind tunnel. It was shown at the 1939 New York World's Fair [7]. |
| Lucky Strike pack (green to white) | 1942 | The background went white and the target logo appeared on both faces. Company president George Washington Hill reportedly bet him $50,000 that he couldn't improve the pack, and paid up [4]. Nationalmuseum Stockholm dates the design to 1942 [5]. |
| Studebaker cars | 1936 onward. First all-new postwar car 1947. Avanti for the 1963 line | Studebaker retained him in 1936. The Avanti was produced on a roughly 40-day design schedule. The 1953 Starliner was credited to Loewy publicly but was actually the work of Robert Bourke [1]. |
| Coca-Cola contour bottle *recast* | 1955 | He was "part of the team" that adapted the existing bottle for King and Family sizes [3]. He did not originate it (see Myths). Wikipedia also credits him with Coca-Cola vending machines (secondary source) [1]. |
| Air Force One livery | 1962 | The blue, white, and chrome scheme is still the basis of US VIP aircraft liveries [1]. |
| Exxon logo | Designed 1966, introduced 1972 | Corporate identity work [1]. |
| Shell pecten | 1971 | The current revision of the logo [6]. |
| NASA Skylab habitability | 1967–1973 | He proposed a shared wardroom and a window, and gave each crew member's sleep compartment a different floor plan for individual identity [1]. |

**Principles**
- **MAYA: "Most Advanced Yet Acceptable."** His estate presents MAYA as his design philosophy [1]. Push a design as far as the audience will accept and no further. *Source note:* Loewy's autobiography *Never Leave Well Enough Alone* was published in 1951 [1]. This pass couldn't confirm that the acronym appears in the book, because the archive.org copy is access-restricted. Cite MAYA as "attributed to Loewy" and don't cite a page number. **[unverified as to the exact 1951 source]**
- Styling sells. Many of his commissions put a new skin over unchanged engineering, as with the Gestetner and the S1 shell [1][7].
- Identity is a system. He worked on logos, packaging, vehicles, and interiors for the same clients, including Studebaker's logo and cars [1].

**Signature moves**
- Streamlining: teardrop and bullet noses, horizontal speed lines, and enclosed machinery [1][7].
- Reduction of a pack or mark to one strong symbol, repeated on every face, as with the Lucky Strike target on both sides of the pack [4].
- Removing a costly element to improve the design. The white Lucky Strike pack dropped the green dye, which was reported to cut printing cost [4].

**Borrow today**
- Apply (product/UI): **novelty budgeting.** In a redesign, hold most patterns typical (navigation position, form behavior, icon meanings) and spend novelty in one or two visible places. Research supports this. Hekkert et al. (2003) found across three studies that people prefer products with an optimal combination of typicality and novelty, and that the two suppress each other's effect when measured alone **[E1; one peer-reviewed paper with three studies on product images; treat it as directional]** [8].
- Apply (brand): stage a rebrand. If the radical version tests poorly, ship an intermediate step and move further in the next cycle. This is MAYA in practice.
- Apply (packaging/social): make one symbol readable from every side and at every crop. The Lucky Strike target on both faces is the model [4].
- Apply (critique): ask "Which parts are advanced, and are they still acceptable to *this* audience?" The answer differs for developers, seniors, and teenagers.

**Limits & critiques**
- Credit inflation. The Loewy office's output was publicly credited to Loewy even when staff did the work (Bourke and the 1953 Starliner) [1]. Don't treat "Loewy designed X" as meaning one person drew it.
- Styling over substance. A new shell can hide unchanged problems. The 1953 Starliner had production problems, and his slanted-front R40 subway car (1967) had to be retrofitted with guard rails for safety [1].
- MAYA is a heuristic, not a law. "Acceptable" changes by audience and decade.

**See also:** `movements-1850-1945.md` (streamlining, Art Deco), `movements-1945-1979.md`; `visual-styles` → `retro-futurism.md`.

## Charles Eames (1907–1978) & Ray Eames (1912–1988) — designers of furniture, film, exhibitions, and method

**Base / discipline / era:** They ran the Eames Office in the Los Angeles area. Their work spans furniture, architecture, film, exhibitions, and toys. They were active from 1941, when they began exploring bent plywood, to 1978 [9]. They were creative partners, though Charles was the office's public face [9]. Credit both.

**Key works**

| Work | Year | Why it matters |
|------|------|----------------|
| LCW (Lounge Chair Wood), molded plywood | 1945–1946 | Molded plywood made affordable, comfortable seating [9]. |
| Molded fiberglass armchair shell | 1948–1950 | They pioneered fiberglass for mass-produced furniture [9]. |
| Eames House (Case Study House No. 8), Pacific Palisades | 1949 | Built from February to December 1949 [9]. Domestic architecture assembled from catalog industrial parts. |
| Eames Lounge Chair and Ottoman (670/671) | 1956 | Designed for Herman Miller. It first appeared on Arlene Francis's TV program *Home* [16]. They wanted "the warm receptive look of a well-used first baseman's mitt" [13]. It combines factory technology with hand craftsmanship [13]. |
| *Mathematica: A World of Numbers... and Beyond* (IBM) | 1961 | An exhibition about mathematics, and the only Eames exhibition still in existence [9]. |
| Design process diagram | 1969 | The designer's, the client's, and society's interests drawn as overlapping areas. Good work happens where all three overlap [11]. |
| Design Q&A | Text dated 1972 by the Eames Office | Short answers on constraints, ethics, and purpose [10]. |
| *Powers of Ten* | 1968 sketch; 1977 final | The 1977 film zooms by factors of ten from a picnic to the cosmos and down to a carbon nucleus. It adapts Kees Boeke's *Cosmic View* (1957) and was added to the US National Film Registry in 1998 [15]. |

**Principles**
- "Design depends largely on constraints." When asked which constraints, they answered "The sum of all constraints." [10]
- "I don't remember ever being forced to accept compromises, but I have willingly accepted constraints." [10]
- Design is "a plan for arranging elements to accomplish a particular purpose" [10]. It is "an expression of purpose," and only later, if good enough, "judged as art" [10].
- "Who would say that pleasure is not useful?" [10]
- "The details are not the details; they make the product." This was written for the script of the Eames film *ECS* [12]. See Myths for the common "they make the design" variant.
- They aimed for "the best for the most for the least" [14].

**Signature moves**
- Iterative prototyping in the office: many full-scale mock-ups before production. The single-shell wood chair took decades to realize [14].
- Using the same idea across media. Furniture, film, exhibitions, and toys all came from one studio.
- Explaining through scale and sequence, as in *Powers of Ten* [15].
- Treating the client as a participant. In the design-diagram commentary, "the client is not the enemy" [11].

**Borrow today**
- Apply (briefs): start every project by listing constraints (price, size, time, platform, accessibility, brand). Treat the list as the design material, not as obstacles [10].
- Apply (strategy/brand): draw the Eames diagram for the project: what the team cares about, what the client needs, and what users and society need. Kill ideas that fall outside the three-way overlap [11].
- Apply (UI/data viz): use the *Powers of Ten* structure for zoomable interfaces such as maps, dashboards, and org charts. Keep one anchor point and change scale in consistent steps, so the user never loses context.
- Apply (critique): the "details make the product" line justifies a pass on empty states, error copy, focus rings, and print finishing [12].
- Apply (motion): a continuous zoom (as in *Powers of Ten*) beats a hard cut when you need to show part-whole relationships.

**Limits & critiques**
- Because Charles was the public face of a joint practice [9], shorthand like "Charles Eames designed..." drops Ray. Write "Charles and Ray Eames" or "the Eames Office" unless a source credits him alone.
- The Lounge Chair's soft, wrinkly leather and down cushions weren't considered "modern" materials when it launched [13]. The famous chair is a luxury object, not a democratic one. Don't use it as an example of "the best for the most for the least."
- Quote hygiene: much "Eames wisdom" online is paraphrase. Use the Q&A text [10] or the Eames Office's own attributions.

**See also:** `movements-1945-1979.md` (mid-century modern), `masters-digital-ux.md` (process and research lineage); `visual-styles` → `scandinavian-nordic.md`, `organic-biophilic.md`.

## Arne Jacobsen (1902–1971) — Danish architect of total design

**Base / discipline / era:** Born in Copenhagen on 11 February 1902 and died there on 24 March 1971 [17]. He was an architect and designer, and worked mainly with Fritz Hansen from 1934 [17].

**Key works**

| Work | Year | Why it matters |
|------|------|----------------|
| Ant chair | Designed 1951–1952 | Made for the Novo pharmaceutical factory canteen. Its seat is form-molded laminated veneer, originally on three legs. Novo's order of 300 chairs persuaded Fritz Hansen to produce it [17][18]. |
| Series 7 chair | 1955 | Light, compact, and stackable [17]. |
| AJ flatware | 1957 | Stanley Kubrick used it in *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968) for its "futuristic" appearance [17]. |
| Egg and Swan chairs | 1958 | Created for the SAS Royal Hotel [17]. Fritz Hansen says the Egg was designed for the hotel's lobby and reception areas [20]. |
| SAS Royal Hotel, Copenhagen | Opened 1 July 1960 | Jacobsen designed the entire hotel, "from the exterior façade through to the stainless-steel cutlery." One room is preserved in the original design [19]. |
| St Catherine's College, Oxford | 1960s | "Again Jacobsen designed everything, including the garden" [17]. |
| Faucets (Vola), after the National Bank competition | From 1961 | Still in production [17]. |
| Cylinda-Line (Stelton) | 1967 | A stainless-steel tableware system [17]. |

**Principles**
- Total design (Gesamtkunstwerk): one designer controls building, furniture, fittings, and cutlery [17][19].
- Thinness and lightness. Fritz Hansen records his studio phrase "as thin as possible and never in the middle" [20].
- Detail as the main act. He worked from meticulous, hand-painted watercolor sketches, and he once designed a single teaspoon [20].

**Signature moves**
- Organic, single-shell seating forms in bent veneer or upholstered foam (Ant, Series 7, Egg, Swan) [17].
- Extending one formal language from architecture down to the smallest object (SAS Royal, St Catherine's) [17][19].
- Systems of related pieces: cutlery ranges and the Cylinda-Line tableware family [17].

**Borrow today**
- Apply (brand/design system): treat every touchpoint as part of one hotel. The 404 page, invoice PDF, packaging tape, and email footer get the same care and the same tokens as the homepage.
- Apply (UI): define a "family resemblance" rule, such as a shared corner radius, stroke weight, and motion curve. A new component should then look like a sibling of existing ones, as the Ant led to the Series 7.
- Apply (environmental/OOH): in retail or event design, specify the small items (signage fixings, cups, wristbands). Jacobsen specified the cutlery [19].
- Apply (critique): ask "Which surface did nobody design?" and fix it first.

**Limits & critiques**
- Total control is costly and slow. Fritz Hansen describes him as "a notoriously difficult man to work with" and a perfectionist whose revisions delayed production [20].
- Some modernist work was rejected in its time. Aarhus City Hall was "deemed too modern," and he had to add a tower and marble cladding [17]. Local protests killed his Bellevue lookout tower [17].
- Don't confuse total design with sameness. Jacobsen's pieces vary in form while sharing a logic.

**See also:** `movements-1945-1979.md`; `visual-styles` → `scandinavian-nordic.md`, `retro-futurism.md` (*2001* aesthetic).

## Achille Castiglioni (1918–2002), with Pier Giacomo Castiglioni (1913–1968) — Italian designer of wit and found objects

**Base / discipline / era:** Born in Milan on 16 February 1918 and died there on 2 December 2002 [22]. He began designing products with his brothers Livio (1911–1979) and Pier Giacomo in 1940. He led the studio with Pier Giacomo until Pier Giacomo died in 1968, then alone until 2002 [22]. He co-founded ADI (the Italian Association for Industrial Design) in 1956 and taught at Turin and the Politecnico di Milano [22].

**Key works**

| Work | Year | Why it matters |
|------|------|----------------|
| Mezzadro stool (with Pier Giacomo; Zanotta) | 1957 | A stamped metal tractor seat used as a stool [21]. |
| Sella stool (Zanotta) | 1957 | Built around a bicycle saddle [21]. |
| Arco floor lamp (with Pier Giacomo; Flos) | 1962 | A pendant on a cantilevered stainless-steel arc anchored by a Carrara marble block. It gives overhead light without a ceiling fixture and has been in continuous production since 1962 [23]. |
| Toio floor lamp (Flos) | 1962 | Uses an automobile headlamp and a transformer, reflecting the ready-made concept associated with Marcel Duchamp [21]. |
| Taccia (1962), Splügen Bräu (1964), Snoopy (1967) lamps (Flos) | 1962–1967 | A lighting series with strong individual characters [21]. |
| RR 126 radiogram (Brionvega) | 1964 | An icon of postwar Italian design [21]. |
| Compasso d'Oro awards | 1955–1984, plus a 1989 special mention | Eight awards, including Luminator (1955), Spinamatic (1964), Parentesi (1979), and Dry cutlery (1984) [22]. |

**Principles**
- Humility over experience: "Experience does not bring certainty or security, but rather increases the possibility of error. I would say it is better to start from scratch each time, with humility." [22]
- Curiosity first. Wikipedia reports he told students "If you are not curious, forget it" (secondary source) [21].
- Redesign from found parts: start from an existing object that already works (a tractor seat, a bicycle saddle, a headlamp) and change its context [21].

**Signature moves**
- The readymade: an industrial component used visibly and unchanged as the core of a new object (Mezzadro, Sella, Toio) [21].
- Structural wit. The Arco's marble counterweight and long arc make the engineering readable [23].
- Character from function. Lamps get names and personalities (Snoopy), but every element still does a job [21].

**Borrow today**
- Apply (UI): compose new features from existing, proven components before inventing new ones. A familiar control in a new context reads as clever, not confusing. This is MAYA's typicality half (see Loewy).
- Apply (brand/illustration): build marks from recognizable found forms (a tool, a ticket, a road sign) re-contextualized, rather than abstract shapes that need explanation.
- Apply (editorial/print): visible structure as ornament. Let the grid, folds, or binding become the expressive element, as the Arco's arc and base do.
- Apply (critique): ask whether there is an existing object or pattern this design should borrow instead of reinventing.

**Limits & critiques**
- Credit the brothers. Arco and Mezzadro are joint Achille and Pier Giacomo works [21][23]. Writing "Castiglioni" alone erases Pier Giacomo.
- The readymade is a joke that only works once per object. Repeated, it becomes novelty décor.
- Iconic designs attract copies, and the Arco has been the subject of copyright litigation (Flos v. Semeraro, 2006 onward) [23]. Don't base a product on a recognizable icon.

**See also:** `movements-1945-1979.md` (Italian design, radical design); `visual-styles` → `playful-friendly.md`.

## Ettore Sottsass (1917–2007) — from Olivetti computers to Memphis

**Base / discipline / era:** Born in Innsbruck on 14 September 1917 and died in Milan on 31 December 2007 [24]. He was an architect and designer. He was a consultant to Olivetti's electronics division for almost thirty years, from 1958 according to the Design Museum (Wikipedia says Adriano Olivetti hired him in 1956) [24][27]. He then led radical design, Memphis, and Sottsass Associati (founded 1980) [24].

**Key works**

| Work | Year | Why it matters |
|------|------|----------------|
| Olivetti Elea 9003 mainframe (industrial design) | Prototype 1958, announced 1959 | Mario Tchou's research group engineered it, and Sottsass did the industrial design. Its cabinets were about one meter high, linked by overhead conduits [26]. The Design Museum credits it with the 1959 Compasso d'Oro [27]. Wikipedia's Elea article ties that award to the Elea 9002, so the sources conflict [26]. |
| "Superboxes" (radical design) | 1960s | Wardrobe-like objects that anticipated Memphis [24]. |
| Olivetti Valentine typewriter | Introduced on Valentine's Day 1969 | Glossy red ABS, with Perry A. King and Albert Leclerc [25]. It made an office machine a personal, portable object. The design is in collections at MoMA, the Met, Cooper Hewitt, the V&A, and the Design Museum [25]. |
| Memphis Group | Founded December 1980; first show Milan 1981; disbanded 1987 | Postmodern furniture with laminates, clashing color, and irony [24][27][28]. |
| Carlton room divider/bookcase (Memphis) | 1981 | The emblematic Memphis object [24]. |

**Principles**
- "Design should also be sensual and exciting." [27]
- Products have emotional and social roles, not just functions. He wanted the Valentine to have an image that would "prevail over the global image of Olivetti" [25].
- Against pure consumerism. Of his radical period: "I didn't want to do any more consumerist products." [24]

**Signature moves**
- Saturated color on technology (the Valentine's red) [25].
- Laminates, patterns, and clashing geometric color blocks (Memphis) [28].
- Totemic, stacked compositions that read as characters (Carlton) [24].
- Ergonomic and human-scale thinking in early computing (the Elea's low cabinets) [26].

**Borrow today**
- Apply (product/UI): give a utilitarian tool one emotional move, such as a signature color, a playful empty state, or a friendly onboarding. The Valentine's red is the model.
- Apply (brand): when a category is visually homogeneous (all blue fintech, all white SaaS), a Memphis-like break in color and pattern buys recognition. Do it only if the brand can sustain it. See `visual-styles` → `memphis.md`, `maximalism.md`.
- Apply (campaign/print/OOH): the Valentine launch ran across posters, radio, magazines, and cinema spots, including a Milton Glaser poster [25]. Design the campaign as one idea across channels.
- Apply (critique): ask "What does this feel like to own or use, not just to operate?"

**Limits & critiques**
- Sottsass disowned the Valentine as "too obvious, a bit like a girl wearing a very short skirt and too much make-up" and said it "came out a mistake" [25]. Its launch met a largely unreceptive market [25]. An icon in hindsight isn't the same as a success at launch.
- Memphis was mocked in its own time. Critics called it a "shotgun wedding between Bauhaus and Fisher-Price," and Terence Conran called it "joke junk" [28]. It is fashion and ages fast. Use it as a time-boxed statement, not a permanent system.
- Dates vary by source: Memphis's founding meeting is given as 6 December 1980 [28] or 11 December 1980 [24], and Olivetti's hiring as 1956 [24] or 1958 [27]. Say "December 1980" and "late 1950s" unless you need the exact day.

**See also:** `movements-1980-present.md` (Memphis, postmodernism), `movements-1945-1979.md` (radical design); `visual-styles` → `memphis.md`, `maximalism.md`, `anti-design-ugly.md`.

## Dieter Rams (1932–present) — Braun, Vitsœ, and the Ten Principles

**Base / discipline / era:** Born in Wiesbaden on 20 May 1932 [31]. He joined Braun in 1955 and became head of design in 1961 according to Vitsœ (1962 according to the Design Museum) [30][32]. He was design director until 1995 and retired from Braun in 1997 [32]. He has worked with Vitsœ since 1959 [30].

**Key works**

| Work | Year | Why it matters |
|------|------|----------------|
| Braun SK 4 radio-phonograph (with Hans Gugelot) | 1956 | A metal body with a clear Plexiglas lid instead of the usual wood. It was nicknamed "Snow White's coffin" [31][32]. Core77 also credits Herbert Lindinger [34]. |
| Braun T3 pocket radio | 1958 | A slim pocket transistor radio [32][33], developed in conjunction with the Ulm school [34]. |
| Vitsœ 606 Universal Shelving System | Development from 1957; dated 1960 by Vitsœ | A modular system of interchangeable shelves, cabinets, and tracks, still in production [29][30][32]. |
| Vitsœ 620 Chair Programme | 1962 | Vitsœ's example for "long-lasting" [29]. |
| Braun ET 66 calculator (**designed by Dietrich Lubs**) | 1987 | Vitsœ itself credits it to Dietrich Lubs, not Rams [29]. It is often misattributed (see Myths). |
| *Weniger, aber besser* / *Less but Better* (book) | 1995 (Jo Klatt Design+Design Verlag, Hamburg; some listings give 1994); revised edition Gestalten 2014 | His own statement of method [35][36]. |
| Ten principles for good design | Formulated against the backdrop of the late 1970s | Vitsœ says that in the late 1970s Rams asked himself "is my design good design?" The principles are his answer [29]. |

**Principles**
- The Ten Principles, with headings verbatim from Vitsœ [29]: "Good design is innovative" · "Good design makes a product useful" · "Good design is aesthetic" · "Good design makes a product understandable" · "Good design is unobtrusive" · "Good design is honest" · "Good design is long-lasting" · "Good design is thorough down to the last detail" · "Good design is environmentally-friendly" · "Good design is as little design as possible."
- "Less, but better – because it concentrates on the essential aspects, and the products are not burdened with non-essentials." [29] The German phrase *Weniger, aber besser* is the title of his book [35].
- "Question everything generally thought to be obvious." [32]
- The late-1970s problem statement: "An impenetrable confusion of forms, colours and noises." [29]

**Signature moves**
- Light gray and white bodies, with color reserved for function (an orange or green switch, or a colored key that marks the most important action).
- Grids of perforations and controls aligned on a strict layout. The control layout explains the product ("it can make the product talk") [29].
- Modularity and systems (606, the 620 programme, Braun's component hi-fi) [29][32].
- Removing until it breaks, then adding back one element.

**Borrow today**
- Apply (design review): use the [checklist below](#rams-ten-principles-as-a-critique-checklist) as the agenda for any critique.
- Apply (UI): reserve color for state and action. A neutral interface with one accent color for the primary action is the Braun switch logic on screen.
- Apply (design systems): build modular components with a fixed grid and few variants. Add a variant only when an existing one demonstrably fails, as 606 grows by adding standard parts.
- Apply (brand/print): "as little design as possible" means fewer typefaces and colors, and no decoration that doesn't carry information.
- Apply (sustainability): "long-lasting" on the web means avoiding trend-driven redesigns, writing timeless copy, and building performant, low-weight pages that stay maintainable.

**Apple–Braun lineage (factual version)**
- Documented connections: Jony Ive wrote the foreword to the 2011 monograph *Dieter Rams: As Little Design as Possible*. In Gary Hustwit's film *Objectified* (2009), Rams named Apple as one of the few companies designing by his principles [31].
- Visual comparisons: the original iPod is often said to closely resemble the T3 [31]. Core77 calls the "inspiration" claim "naively touted by the blogosphere," noting that comparison photos rotate and resize the T3 [34]. Wikipedia says Apple's iOS calculator "references" the ET 66 [31]. The ET 66 is by Dietrich Lubs [29].
- What to say: "Apple's design team publicly admired Rams, and several Apple products visibly echo Braun objects." Don't say "Apple copied Rams" or "Rams designed the calculator that inspired iOS." This pass located no Apple primary source naming specific Braun objects as sources.

**Limits & critiques**
- "As little design as possible" is often misread as "remove everything." Rams' principles also demand usefulness, understandability, and aesthetics [29]. Minimalism that hides function fails principles 2 and 4.
- Neutral gray restraint can read as cold or generic when copied without the craft. See `visual-styles` → `minimalism.md` for when it ages badly.
- Many "Rams" products were team work at Braun (Gugelot, Lubs, Greubel, Lindinger). Credit the team [29][34].

**See also:** `movements-1945-1979.md` (Ulm, Braun functionalism), `masters-digital-ux.md`; `visual-styles` → `minimalism.md`, `swiss-international.md`, `apple-style.md`.

## Rams' Ten Principles as a critique checklist

Use this table as a review agenda. Each principle heading is Rams' own wording [29], shared under Vitsœ's CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 terms. The questions are this plugin's translation for screens, posters, and brands. They are not Rams' text.

| # | Principle (Rams) | Critic's question | Screen (UI/web) check | Poster / brand check |
|---|------------------|-------------------|-----------------------|----------------------|
| 1 | Good design is innovative | Does any novelty here solve a real problem, or is it novelty for its own sake? | Is the new interaction measurably better than the platform convention it replaces? | Is the new visual idea tied to the message, or a trend swap? |
| 2 | Good design makes a product useful | What is the main job, and does every element support it? | Can a first-time user complete the main task without detours? | Does the poster state what, when, where, and how in the first read? |
| 3 | Good design is aesthetic | Is it well executed: alignment, spacing, type, color, finishing? | Are spacing, type scale, and color tokens applied consistently? | Do the type setting, kerning, and print finishing hold up at full size? |
| 4 | Good design makes a product understandable | Does the structure explain itself without help text? | Do labels, hierarchy, and affordances tell users what to do? | Is the hierarchy obvious: what to read first, second, third? |
| 5 | Good design is unobtrusive | Does the design leave room for the user's content and intent? | Does the chrome recede so user content leads? | Does the brand system leave room for campaign content and partners? |
| 6 | Good design is honest | Does it claim more than the product delivers? | Any dark patterns: fake urgency, hidden costs, pre-checked boxes, disguised ads? | Are claims, imagery, and "before/after" visuals truthful? |
| 7 | Good design is long-lasting | Will this look dated in three years? | Is the style tied to a passing trend (heavy glass blur, gradients)? Can tokens evolve without a rebuild? | Will the mark work in five years and in one color? |
| 8 | Good design is thorough down to the last detail | Which states and edge cases were never designed? | Check empty, loading, error, offline, long-string, RTL, zoomed, and keyboard focus states. | Check small sizes, embroidery, favicon, dark backgrounds, and the bleed. |
| 9 | Good design is environmentally-friendly | What does it consume, and what does it waste? | Check page weight, autoplay video, and needless re-renders. Is there a dark mode for OLED? | Check paper stock, ink coverage, print runs, and disposable merch. |
| 10 | Good design is as little design as possible | What can be removed without loss? | Remove one element per screen and test whether anything breaks. | Remove one typeface, one color, or one graphic device and compare. |

Scoring rule for agents: rate each row Pass / Weak / Fail with one sentence of evidence. Any Fail on rows 2, 4, 6, or 8 blocks sign-off. Rows 1, 7, and 10 are judgment calls and need a rationale, not a block.

## Jony Ive (1967–present) — Apple's design chief, then LoveFrom

**Base / discipline / era:** Born in Chingford, London, on 27 February 1967 [37]. He was at Apple from 1992 (full-time from September 1992) to 2019. He became SVP of Industrial Design in 1997, took on leadership of Human Interface in October 2012, and was Chief Design Officer from May 2015 [37]. He founded LoveFrom in 2019 [37].

**Key works**

| Work | Year | Why it matters |
|------|------|----------------|
| iMac G3 | Unveiled 6 May 1998; shipped 15 August 1998 | A teardrop-shaped translucent "Bondi blue" case with a handle and no floppy drive. It was Apple's fastest-selling computer at the time [38]. |
| iPod | 2001 | Ive was on the original design team [37]. |
| iPhone | 2007 | Its form factor is credited to Ive [37]. |
| Unibody MacBook Pro | Announced 14 October 2008 | The case is machined from a single piece of aluminum. That manufacturing process became a design language [44]. |
| iOS 7 | Announced 10 June 2013; released 18 September 2013 | Moved iOS away from skeuomorphism toward flatter icons, slimmer fonts, translucency, and layers [39][40]. |
| Departure from Apple | Announced 27 June 2019 | Apple said it would be a primary client of his new firm [41]. |
| LoveFrom (with Marc Newson) | 2019 to present | Clients and projects include the King Charles III coronation emblem (2023) and Ferrari (partnership 2021; the Luce car shown in 2026) [37]. |
| io → OpenAI | io founded 2024; merger announced 21 May 2025; completed 9 July 2025 | Ive co-founded io with Scott Cannon, Evans Hankey, and Tang Tan. After the merger, Ive and LoveFrom stay independent and "assume deep design and creative responsibilities across OpenAI" [42]. The deal was reported at $6.5 billion [37]. |

**Status (Last verified 2026-10):** In January 2026, OpenAI's Chris Lehane said the company was "on track" to unveil its first device in the second half of 2026. He didn't commit to it going on sale that year [43]. This pass found no confirmed public unveiling or launch as of October 2026 **[unverified; re-check before citing]**.

**Principles**
- "There is a profound and enduring beauty in simplicity, in clarity, in efficiency. True simplicity is derived from so much more than just the absence of clutter and ornamentation—it's about bringing order to complexity." (iOS 7 launch, 2013) [39]
- Manufacturing process as design language. The unibody enclosure is the clearest case [44].
- Hardware and software as one design. In 2012 he took responsibility for Human Interface alongside Industrial Design [37].

**Signature moves**
- Translucent and colored plastics to make technology friendly (iMac G3) [38].
- Fewer seams, fewer parts, and continuous surfaces (unibody) [44].
- Restraint plus one material story per product (aluminum, glass, colored plastic).
- In UI: removing skeuomorphic texture and expressing hierarchy through layers, translucency, and motion (iOS 7) [39].

**Borrow today**
- Apply (UI/design systems): define hierarchy through layering, such as surface levels, translucency, and depth on interaction, rather than decorative texture. Keep contrast accessible (see Limits).
- Apply (brand/product marketing): let the making process be the story, such as the code, the craft, or the supply chain. Apple sold "unibody" as a design idea [44].
- Apply (cross-platform): one team, or one set of tokens, should own the hardware, packaging, and UI language, so the product feels authored by one hand.
- Apply (critique): ask "Does this bring order to complexity, or just remove visible clutter?" [39]

**Limits & critiques**
- iOS 7 got a mixed reception at launch. Critics cited animations that delayed access, inconsistent icons, and hidden buttons. Some users reported motion sickness from the zoom and parallax effects [40]. Flat, thin, translucent UI needs contrast and reduced-motion support. See `visual-styles` → `flat-design.md`, `glassmorphism.md`.
- Don't read every Apple product as one person's authorship. Apple credits teams, and Ive led them.
- Volatile facts: LoveFrom and OpenAI device status change. Carry a "Last verified" date whenever you cite them.

**See also:** `masters-digital-ux.md`, `movements-1980-present.md`; `visual-styles` → `apple-style.md`, `flat-design.md`, `skeuomorphism.md`, `minimalism.md`.

## Naoto Fukasawa (1956–present) — designing from unconscious behavior

**Base / discipline / era:** Born in Yamanashi Prefecture in 1956 [45]. He worked at Seiko Epson from 1980 and at ID Two (now IDEO San Francisco) from 1989. He opened IDEO's Tokyo office in 1996 and founded NAOTO FUKASAWA DESIGN in 2003 [45]. He sits on Muji's design advisory board (from 2002) [45][46] and is one of the directors of 21_21 Design Sight [45].

**Key works**

| Work | Year | Why it matters |
|------|------|----------------|
| "Without Thought" concept and workshops | Concept named after a 1998 exchange with Bill Moggridge and Bill Verplank. Workshops held every year since 1999 | Design should start from people's unconscious behavior [45][47]. |
| Muji wall-mounted CD player | 1999 | Shaped like a kitchen fan, with a pull cord that starts the music. MoMA acquired it, and Muji saw it on display there and put it into production [46][47]. |
| ±0 (Plus Minus Zero) | 2003 | A household-appliance brand built around objects that feel "just right" [46]. |
| INFOBAR phone (KDDI) | 2003 | A tile-keypad phone [46]. |
| Super Normal (with Jasper Morrison) | 2006 exhibition, Axis Gallery Tokyo; book 2007 | 204 everyday objects, from anonymous items to classics, that are "super normal" [45][46][48]. |

**Principles**
- "Design dissolving in behavior." [45]
- The impetus for design lies in people's unconscious behavior, which he named "Without Thought" [45].
- Normality, archetype, and outline. He uses these terms to describe objects whose form people already know [45].

**Signature moves**
- Borrow an existing gesture: if a CD spins like a fan, give it a fan's pull cord [47].
- Archetypal outlines with softened, rounded edges.
- Neutral materials and colors so the object recedes into daily life (the Muji, ±0 register).

**Borrow today**
- Apply (UX research): run "Without Thought" observation. Watch what people already do with no instruction (where thumbs rest, which gestures they use, what they work around), then design the interface to fit that behavior. Use contextual inquiry and diary studies, not just interviews. See `masters-digital-ux.md`.
- Apply (UI): map new actions onto gestures people already perform (pull to refresh, swipe to dismiss) instead of teaching new ones. This is affordance in Norman's sense of perceivable action possibilities. Norman later added "signifiers" to say where the action happens [60].
- Apply (brand/packaging): "super normal" positioning. Make the product the obvious default in its category rather than the loudest.
- Apply (critique): ask "Would someone use this correctly while distracted?"

**Limits & critiques**
- "Normal" depends on culture. Unconscious behavior observed in Tokyo may not transfer, so test locally.
- Super Normal restraint can become bland sameness in brand work. Pair it with one clear distinguishing feature.
- Workshop findings are design inspiration, not validated usability evidence. Test them before relying on them.

**See also:** `masters-digital-ux.md`; `visual-styles` → `minimalism.md`, `scandinavian-nordic.md`.

## Marc Newson (1963–present) — signature form across categories

**Base / discipline / era:** Born in Sydney on 20 October 1963 [49]. He is an industrial designer working in furniture, watches, aviation, cars, and consumer electronics. He was at Apple from 2014 and has been at LoveFrom since 2019 [49].

**Key works**

| Work | Year | Why it matters |
|------|------|----------------|
| LC1 / Lockheed Lounge | LC1 prototype 1986; Lockheed Lounge 1988 (Wikipedia also gives 1988–90) | A riveted aluminum-skinned chaise. It sold for £2.4 million in 2015, which Wikipedia calls the most expensive object ever sold by a living designer [49]. |
| Embryo Chair (Idée, Tokyo) | 1988 | In his words, "one of the first pieces where I hit upon a discernible style" [49]. |
| Ikepod (co-founded) | 1994 (left 2012) | Watches carrying his rounded, pebble-like forms [49]. |
| Ford 021C concept car | 1999 | A product designer's car [49]. |
| Qantas (creative director): Skybed seat, A380 and A330 interiors | 2000s–2013 | Aviation interiors as brand [49]. |
| Apple design team | Joined September 2014 | Part-time, UK-based, hired by Ive for "very specific reasons" [49][50]. |
| LoveFrom | From June 2019 | Co-leads the firm with Ive [49]. |

**Apple Watch involvement:** Wikipedia lists the 2015 Apple Watch among his works but doesn't state his role [49]. In October 2014, asked whether he had worked on the Watch, Newson said "Apparently I can't" (answer), and Apple PR declined to comment [50]. Treat his Apple Watch role as **reported, not officially confirmed**.

**Principles**
- A recognizable formal signature: soft, continuous, "blobby" volumes, pinched waists, and riveted or seamless skins. This is an observed pattern, not a stated manifesto.
- Work across categories with one hand: the same language on a chair, a watch, a seat, or a car [49].

**Signature moves**
- Continuous rounded volumes and porthole openings (Embryo, Ikepod) [49].
- Metal skins that show their fabrication (Lockheed's rivets) [49].
- Bright, saturated single colors on otherwise pure forms.

**Borrow today**
- Apply (brand systems): a single formal signature, such as a corner radius, a pill shape, or a cutout, can unify very different touchpoints (app icon, packaging, signage), as Newson's form unifies furniture and watches.
- Apply (UI): pick one shape language (for example, superellipse corners and pill buttons) and apply it everywhere, including illustrations and charts.
- Apply (premium/luxury): show the making, through rivets, seams, and material honesty. See `visual-styles` → `luxury-premium.md`.
- Apply (critique): ask "Could you recognize this as ours with the logo removed?"

**Limits & critiques**
- Signature form can become self-parody, or can override function when forced onto unsuitable products.
- Gallery-priced limited editions like the Lockheed Lounge aren't evidence of usability or mass appeal [49].

**See also:** `movements-1980-present.md`; `visual-styles` → `luxury-premium.md`, `retro-futurism.md`, `apple-style.md`.

## Hella Jongerius (1963–present) — color, material, and the imperfect industrial

**Base / discipline / era:** Born in De Meern, Netherlands, on 30 May 1963 [51]. She studied at Design Academy Eindhoven (1988–1993) and founded Jongeriuslab in Rotterdam in 1993, moving it to Berlin in 2008 [51]. She works in textiles, ceramics, furniture, interiors, and color research.

**Key works**

| Work | Year | Why it matters |
|------|------|----------------|
| Polder sofa (Vitra) | 2005 | A mix of fabrics, colors, industrial elements, and craft details, named for the Dutch reclaimed-land landscape. It was her first industrially designed furniture and began her collaboration with Vitra [52]. |
| Art direction of colours and materials for Vitra ("Inside Colours" show) | 2007–2017 (exhibition); library work 2007–2020 | Her color research for Vitra. Her role was Art Director for Colours and Surfaces, developing Vitra's color and surface library from 2007 until 2020 [53][54]. |
| Vitra Colour & Material Library (color wheel system) | Colourwheel 2011; library 2015 | An open, organic color-wheel structure for organizing a commercial palette [54]. |
| KLM World Business Class cabin | 2011 (cabin interior); 2013 (seat, palette, products) | The concept was "simplification," "softening," and "enrichment." On the seat, KLM blue appears when the bed is fully flat [55][56]. Wikipedia dates the 747 work to 2012 [51]. |
| UN North Delegates' Lounge, New York | 2013 | With Rem Koolhaas, Irma Boom, Gabriel Lester, and Louise Schouwenberg. Includes the Knots & Beads curtain (30,000 porcelain beads) and new Vitra furniture. The redesign was a gift from the Netherlands [57]. |
| *Beyond the New* manifesto (with Louise Schouwenberg) | 2015 (Milan); exhibition 2017, Die Neue Sammlung | Calls for "new ideals in design, a holistic approach on all levels" [51][59]. |
| *I Don't Have a Favourite Colour* (book) | 2016 | About the Vitra Colour and Material Library [51]. |
| *Breathing Colour* exhibition | Design Museum London, 2017; toured to Rotterdam 2018 and Stockholm 2019 | Shows how color changes with shape, texture, and daylight. Argues that industrialization "narrowed our experiences of colour" [58]. |

**Principles**
- Colors aren't fixed codes. They change with light, form, and material [58].
- A commercial palette must work in practice. Her own account says it should offer "a white, black, some greys and browns, and a red and green," leaving only a few colors to create character [54].
- Against short-lived products: "There's too much shit design" (Design Week interview, 2015, as reported by Wikipedia) [51].
- Combine opposites: industrial production with handwork and imperfection [51][52].

**Signature moves**
- Mixed fabrics and visible craft details on industrial furniture (Polder buttons and textiles) [52].
- Color organized as a wheel or open system, not a fixed list [54].
- Color revealed in use (the KLM blue that appears when the seat is flat) [56].
- Editing an existing space instead of replacing it (the UN Lounge kept iconic Scandinavian pieces and existing art) [57].

**Borrow today**
- Apply (design systems/color): build palettes as relationships (a wheel with roles and pairings), not as a flat list of hex codes. Include the practical neutrals her palette rule requires [54]. Test every token under real conditions: dark mode, outdoor screens, different display gamuts, and print stocks. This is the screen version of *Breathing Colour* [58].
- Apply (brand/print): specify colors with material and light in mind. The same brand red on uncoated paper, fabric, and OLED needs separate values.
- Apply (UI): reveal color in states. For example, a calm neutral UI where the brand color appears on success, completion, or the "flat-bed" moment.
- Apply (redesigns): edit, don't erase. Keep the recognizable assets and add new ones around them, as at the UN Lounge [57].

**Limits & critiques**
- Craft and imperfection cues can read as costly or precious, so match them to the brand's price point.
- Color "breathing" is an artistic and phenomenological claim, not a measured perception effect **[E3 as a perception claim]**. Use WCAG contrast checks for accessibility regardless.
- Title check: the color exhibition is *Breathing Colour*, not "Breaking Colour," and the book is *I Don't Have a Favourite Colour* [51][58].

**See also:** `movements-1980-present.md` (Droog, Dutch design); `visual-styles` → `organic-biophilic.md`, `scandinavian-nordic.md`, `luxury-premium.md`.

## Cross-cutting lessons

| Lesson | Who shows it | Apply to digital and graphic work |
|--------|--------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Novelty is a budget.** Spend it in one or two places, and keep everything else typical. | Loewy (MAYA) [1]; Hekkert et al. **[E1, limited]** [8]; Castiglioni's familiar parts [21] | In redesigns, keep navigation and core flows typical and put the new idea in the hero, the brand moment, or one signature interaction. |
| **Constraints are the material.** | Eames [10] | Write the constraint list (platform, accessibility, budget, deadline, brand) before sketching, and judge concepts against it. |
| **Honest materials, honest UI.** | Rams principle 6 [29]; Jongerius's visible craft [52]; Newson's rivets [49] | No fake buttons, no faux-3D that implies a pressable surface where none exists, no dark patterns. Show loading and system state truthfully. |
| **Affordance and signifier.** | Fukasawa's pull cord [47]; Rams's "make the product talk" [29] | Make the action visible and placed where people already reach. Norman separates affordances (what's possible) from signifiers (where to act) [60]. |
| **Total design.** | Jacobsen [17][19]; Ive (hardware plus Human Interface) [37] | One token system and one voice across product, marketing, email, and print. Audit the undesigned surfaces. |
| **Observe unconscious behavior.** | Fukasawa [45] | In UX research, watch actual behavior through contextual observation before you ask opinions. |
| **Color is contextual.** | Jongerius [54][58]; Sottsass's red [25] | Test palettes in context (devices, light, materials). Reserve saturated color for meaning. |
| **Durability over fashion.** | Rams [29]; Jongerius, *Beyond the New* [59] | Prefer systems that can evolve over seasonal restyles. Date-stamp trend-driven choices so they can be reviewed. |
| **Credit the team.** | Loewy office (Bourke) [1]; Braun (Lubs, Gugelot) [29][34]; Castiglioni brothers [21] | In case studies and portfolios, name the collaborators. |

## Common misattributions & myths

| Claim | Status | Correct version |
|-------|--------|-----------------|
| "Raymond Loewy designed the Coca-Cola contour bottle." | **False** | The bottle came from the Root Glass Company, Terre Haute, in 1915. Earl R. Dean sketched it, and the patent was in Alexander Samuelson's name. Loewy was part of the team that recast it for larger sizes in 1955 and called it the "perfect liquid wrapper" [2][3]. Wikipedia attributes the myth to misinterpretations of Loewy's comments [2]. |
| "Loewy redesigned the Lucky Strike pack in 1940." | **Date disputed** | Museum and encyclopedic sources date the white pack to 1942 [4][5]. Use 1942. |
| "Loewy designed the 1953 Studebaker Starliner." | **Misleading** | It was publicly credited to Loewy but was actually the work of Robert Bourke in the Loewy studio [1]. |
| "MAYA comes from *Never Leave Well Enough Alone* (1951)." | **Unverified** | MAYA is consistently attributed to Loewy, and his estate promotes it [1]. This pass couldn't confirm the acronym in the 1951 text. Cite it as "attributed to Loewy." |
| "'The details are not the details. They make the design.' — Charles Eames" | **Paraphrase** | The Eames Office gives "The details are not the details; they make the product," written by Charles and Ray Eames for the *ECS* film script [12]. Credit both Eameses. |
| "Charles Eames designed the Lounge Chair / Eames House." | **Incomplete** | These are Charles and Ray Eames works [9]. |
| "Dieter Rams designed the ET 66 calculator that inspired the iPhone calculator." | **False as to designer** | The ET 66 (1987) is by Dietrich Lubs for Braun [29]. The iOS resemblance is documented as a reference, not an Apple statement [31]. |
| "The iPod was copied from the Braun T3." | **Overstated** | It is a widely shared visual comparison. Core77 calls the "inspiration" claim naive and notes the photos are rescaled [34]. Documented ties are Ive's admiration and foreword (2011) and Rams's praise of Apple (2009) [31]. |
| "Rams's principles say: remove everything." | **Misreading** | Principle 10 is "as little design as possible," but principles 2 to 4 require usefulness, aesthetics, and understandability [29]. |
| "Sottsass designed the Valentine alone and loved it." | **False** | It was made with Perry A. King and Albert Leclerc. Sottsass later called it "too obvious" and "a mistake" [25]. |
| "Memphis was founded in 1981." | **Imprecise** | It was founded in December 1980 (6 or 11 December, depending on source). The first exhibition was in Milan in 1981 [24][27][28]. |
| "Castiglioni designed Arco alone." | **Incomplete** | Achille and Pier Giacomo Castiglioni designed it together, 1962 [23]. |
| "Marc Newson designed the Apple Watch." | **Unconfirmed** | He joined Apple in September 2014. His Watch role was never officially confirmed, and he declined to answer in 2014 [49][50]. |
| "Jongerius's books *Breaking Colour* / *Beyond Colour*." | **Wrong titles** | *Breathing Colour* is an exhibition (Design Museum London, 2017) [58]. The book is *I Don't Have a Favourite Colour* (2016) [51]. *Beyond the New* is a 2015 manifesto with Louise Schouwenberg [59]. |
| "The OpenAI device by Jony Ive has launched." | **Check date** | As of 2026-10, this pass found only the January 2026 statement of a second-half-2026 unveiling target [43]. Re-verify before citing. |
