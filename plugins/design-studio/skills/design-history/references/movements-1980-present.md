# Design Movements, 1980–Present

> **Scope:** Graphic and digital design movements from postmodernism to today. Covers postmodernism and Basel "New Wave", Memphis, punk and post-punk graphics, US New Wave, grunge and "deconstruction", and the digital eras: early web, Flash, Web 2.0, skeuomorphism to flat, responsive, design systems, and the 2020s (neo-brutalism, glassmorphism and Liquid Glass, variable fonts, generative AI). Covers history, the debates, and how to borrow from each today.
> **Last verified:** 2026-10 (platform and AI facts are volatile; each carries its verification context)
> **Used by:** creative-director, art-director, design-critic, identity-designer, typographer, editorial-designer, ui-designer, ux-researcher, design-system-architect, interaction-motion, print-ooh-designer, brand-strategist

**How to use this file:** Read the timeline. Open the section whose *problem* matches the brief, then follow the `visual-styles` links for token recipes. This file covers history, debates, and dated milestones. People get short mentions; full profiles live in `masters-graphic-contemporary.md`, `masters-digital-ux.md`, `masters-product-industrial.md`, and `masters-type-designers.md`. Pre-1980 roots (Swiss style, the Crouwel–van Toorn debate of 1972) are in `movements-1945-1979.md`.

## Postmodernism (1966 text; c. 1970s–1990s practice; Philadelphia, Basel, Milan, Cranbrook)

**Context: why it happened**
- Architecture led the theory. In *Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture* (1966), Robert Venturi argued against modernism's simple diagrammatic forms and for "the difficult whole." He called the book a "gentle manifesto" [1].
- Venturi coined "Less is a bore" as a postmodern counter to Mies van der Rohe's "Less is more" [1].
- By the late 1960s, the Swiss style was the corporate default (see `movements-1945-1979.md`). Its younger practitioners wanted expression, history, and ambiguity back.
- Literary theory (semiotics, post-structuralism) entered design schools, especially Cranbrook from the late 1970s [5][7].

**Key figures and works**

| Person | Work | Year | Significance |
|--------|------|------|--------------|
| Robert Venturi | *Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture* | 1966 | The founding text of postmodern design attitudes [1]. |
| Robert Venturi | Vanna Venturi House, Philadelphia | 1964 | Broken-gable icon of postmodern architecture [1]. |
| Venturi, Denise Scott Brown, Steven Izenour | *Learning from Las Vegas* (from a 1968 Yale studio) | 1972 | The "duck" vs. "decorated shed": the commercial strip as a sign system [1]. |
| Wolfgang Weingart (1941–2021) | Typography teaching at the Basel Kunstgewerbeschule | From 1968 | The "father" of New Wave / Swiss Punk typography [2][3]. |
| Wolfgang Weingart | Editorial board, *Typographische Monatsblätter* | 1970–1988 | A platform for the Basel experiments [2]. |
| Wolfgang Weingart | US lecture tour | Early 1970s | Led many American designers to study in Basel [3]. |
| Dan Friedman | Taught at Yale from 1969; studied with Weingart | 1969– | Replaced "legibility" with the broader "readability" [3]. |
| Katherine McCoy (b. 1945) with Michael McCoy | Co-chairs, Cranbrook Academy of Art graduate design | 1971–1995 | Brought semiotics, post-structuralism, and literary theory into the studio [5]. |
| Cranbrook | *Cranbrook Design: The New Discourse* (Rizzoli) | 1991 | Documented 1980s studio work. Wikipedia says it "probably sealed the reputation" of the school [5]. |

**Manifestos and key texts (dated)**
- 1966: Venturi, *Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture* [1].
- 1972: Venturi, Scott Brown, and Izenour, *Learning from Las Vegas* [1].
- 9 November 1972: the Crouwel–van Toorn debate (rational vs. personal). It is the European prelude to the postmodern legibility arguments. See `movements-1945-1979.md`.
- 2000: Weingart, *Typography: My Way to Typography* (Lars Müller). Weingart: "I never intended to create a 'style'." [2]

**Visual DNA (graphic postmodernism)**
- Type: Swiss grotesques, now letterspaced wide, stepped, set at angles, and mixed in weight within a word [3].
- Layout: hierarchy that departs from top-down reading; text centered, ragged left, or chaotic [3].
- Texture: transparent film layers and collage [3].
- Color: more type sizes and colors, more ornament [3]. Pastels next to black in the US variant.
- Imagery: historical quotation; vernacular signs (Las Vegas) [1].
- Production technology: phototypesetting, film positives, offset; the Macintosh from 1984 (see US New Wave below).

**Apply today**
- **Use when:** a brand needs layered meaning, cultural wit, or a visible break from corporate rationalism. Suits museums, festivals, fashion, and publishing.
- **Tokens:**
  - One grotesque (Space Grotesk, Archivo) with letter-spacing from +0.1 to +0.3em on labels.
  - Rotated text blocks at 15 to 90 degrees.
  - Stepped rules: 1px lines offset at 8px increments.
  - Pastel-plus-black palette, e.g., pink `#F7A8B8` behind black text `#111111` (10.11:1), mint `#9BE3C7` behind `#111111` (12.77:1), cobalt `#2E3BFF` with white text (6.59:1) [45].
- **Typefaces (Google Fonts, verified) [44]:** Space Grotesk, a proportional sans derived from Colophon's Space Mono (2016), "originally designed by Florian Karsten in 2018."
- Related: `visual-styles` → `maximalism.md` (Venturi's "Less is a bore" is its creed), `editorial-magazine-web.md`, `kinetic-typography.md`.

**Pitfalls**
- **Style without the argument.** Lupton warns that "deconstruction" became "the tag for yet another period style" [7]. The same happened to postmodernism. Know what the layering says.
- **Readability debt.** Rotated and letterspaced text in UI fails scanning and localization. Keep experiments in display and editorial layers.
- **Myth:** "Weingart invented a style." In his words, he never intended to [2].

**Legacy**
- Opened the door to Memphis, Emigre, and Cranbrook (below).
- *Learning from Las Vegas* is the ancestor of every "vernacular" and "anti-design" revival (`visual-styles` → `anti-design-ugly.md`).

## Memphis Group (1980–c.1987/88; Milan)

Focus here is history. For tokens see `visual-styles` → `memphis.md`; for Sottsass and the product side see `masters-product-industrial.md`.

**Context: why it happened**
- Ettore Sottsass gathered young designers and architects on 6 December 1980 [12]. The name came partly from Bob Dylan's record playing that night, and partly from the double reference: the Tennessee city of the blues and the ancient Egyptian capital [12].
- It was a reaction against modernist "good taste." Plastic laminate was chosen precisely because it read as cheap [12][13].
- Sottsass had already brought pop color into industry with the Olivetti *Valentine* (1969). See `movements-1945-1979.md`.

**Key figures and works**

| Person / group | Work | Year | Significance |
|----------------|------|------|--------------|
| Ettore Sottsass and group | First Memphis exhibition, Arc '74 gallery, Milan | 18 September 1981 | Made the group internationally famous [13]. |
| Members and contributors | Martine Bedin, Andrea Branzi, Aldo Cibic, Michele De Lucchi, Nathalie Du Pasquier, Michael Graves, Hans Hollein, Arata Isozaki, Shiro Kuramata, Javier Mariscal, Alessandro Mendini, Peter Shire, George Sowden, Matteo Thun, Masanori Umeda, Barbara Radice and others | 1980s | An international roster around Milan [12][13]. |
| Sottsass | Leaves the group | 1985 | [12] |
| The group | Disbands | 1987 [12] or 1988 [13] | Sources disagree. Write "c. 1987–88". |

**Manifestos and key texts**
- No single manifesto is verified in this pass. The 1981 Arc '74 show functioned as the declaration [13].
- Critics were divided. Terence Conran dismissed the work as "joke junk" [12].

**Visual DNA (summary; tokens in `memphis.md`)**
- Patterned plastic laminate and terrazzo; bright clashing color; abstract decoration; asymmetric, toy-like shapes [12].
- Production technology: laminate over particleboard, lacquered wood and metal, glass, ceramic [13].

**Apply today**
- Use for retail, youth, kids, music, and food brands that need to look deliberately "wrong" and joyful.
- Pull the full token set from `visual-styles` → `memphis.md`.
- Keep pattern in decorative surfaces and backgrounds. Keep text on solid fields.

**Pitfalls**
- **Squiggle pastiche.** Random squiggles and confetti are the 2010s revival, not Memphis. The original used structural asymmetry and furniture-scale pattern.
- **Myth: "Memphis was a graphic style."** It was a furniture, lighting, textile, and object collective [12]. The graphic look is a derivative.

**Legacy**
- Spread through popular culture from the mid-1980s to mid-1990s.
- Later quoted by fashion houses (Dior, Missoni) [12].
- Revived in 2010s graphic design and absorbed into vaporwave (see `memphis.md`).

## New Wave and punk-era graphics (UK 1976–1983; US 1976–1986; London, Manchester, Los Angeles)

"New Wave" means two different things. In typography, it means the Basel-derived approach (Weingart) and its US carriers. In music graphics, it means post-punk record design. Both are covered here because they shared a moment.

**Context: why it happened**
- In Basel, Weingart's experiments from 1968 reached the US through his early-1970s lecture tour and through students who trained in Basel [3].
- In the UK, punk wanted cheap, fast, oppositional graphics that looked like they came from the street, not the agency.
- Post-punk labels such as Factory Records (Manchester, co-founded 1978 by Tony Wilson, Alan Erasmus, and Peter Saville [15]) answered punk's chaos with cool, modernist-quoting restraint.
- The Macintosh (1984) gave the US New Wave a new tool. April Greiman saw it demonstrated by Alan Kay at the first TED Conference in February 1984 and bought one [4].

**Key figures and works**

| Person | Work | Year | Significance |
|--------|------|------|--------------|
| Jamie Reid (1947–2023) | *Suburban Press* (radical magazine; newspaper-cut lettering) | Founded 1970 | Origin of the "ransom-note" lettering he later used for the Sex Pistols [14]. |
| Jamie Reid | Sex Pistols "God Save the Queen" sleeve; Sex Pistols logo | 1977 | Cecil Beaton's photo of Elizabeth II defaced with a safety pin and swastikas. *The Observer*'s Sean O'Hagan called it "the single most iconic image of the punk era" [14]. |
| Jamie Reid | *Never Mind the Bollocks, Here's the Sex Pistols*; "Pretty Vacant"; "Holidays in the Sun" | 1977 | Punk's visual identity [14]. Authorship of the "Nowhere Buses" graphic on "Pretty Vacant" was disputed in 2010 [14]. |
| Peter Saville (b. 1955) | Joy Division, *Unknown Pleasures* | June 1979 | Pulsar CP 1919 radio-pulse plot from *The Cambridge Encyclopaedia of Astronomy*, originally drawn by Harold Craft for a 1970 dissertation. Saville inverted it to white on black [16]. **Date edge:** the cover falls in 1979; it sits here because it opens the post-punk lineage. |
| Peter Saville | Joy Division, *Closer* | 1980 | Released shortly after Ian Curtis's death, but the design predated it [15]. |
| Peter Saville | New Order, "Blue Monday"; *Power, Corruption & Lies* | 1983 | *PCL* pairs Fantin-Latour's roses with a color-coded alphabet: appropriation across genres [15]. Saville cited Jan Tschichold and the New Typography as influences [15]. |
| April Greiman (b. 1948) | Studied at Basel under Armin Hofmann and Weingart | 1970–71 | Helped bring New Wave to the US with Jayme Odgers [4]. |
| April Greiman | Own studio in Los Angeles; *WET* magazine cover with Odgers | 1976 | West Coast New Wave [4]. |
| April Greiman | Director of design at CalArts; department renamed Visual Communications | 1982; 1984 | Institutional base for the approach [4]. |
| April Greiman | *Design Quarterly* #133, *Does It Make Sense?* (Walker Art Center) | 1986 | A fold-out poster made with Macintosh-digitized video. Wikipedia describes it as countering the "objective, rational and masculine tendencies of modernist design" [4]. |

**Manifestos and key texts**
- None verified as a formal manifesto.
- Reid's sleeves and Greiman's *Design Quarterly* #133 are the declaratory works.

**Visual DNA**
- **Punk:** ransom-note cut letters; photocopy and newsprint textures; defaced official imagery; fluorescent spot colors with black.
- **Factory and post-punk:** found scientific or classical images; modernist type quotations; minimal text on the sleeve (sometimes none); codes instead of words (*PCL*'s color alphabet [15]).
- **US New Wave:** Basel layering plus video stills, early bitmap type, pastel and neon color, diagonal space, a hybrid of photography and type [4].
- **Production technology:** photocopier and letterpress collage (punk); offset with special inks (Factory); early Macintosh and video digitizing (Greiman [4]).

**Apply today**
- **Punk palette [45]:**
  - Fluorescent-style yellow `#FFF200` with black text: 17.95:1.
  - Hot pink `#FF2D95` with black text: 6.06:1.
  - Never set yellow text on pink (2.96:1).
  - Use scanned paper textures at 10–20% opacity, not fake "grunge" brushes.
- **Ransom-note type:** cut real letters and scan them, or set each letter in a different face. Keep the URL, date, and price in one plain face. Special Elite (typewriter, Google Fonts) is a usable "photocopied flyer" body face [44].
- **Factory-style:** one found or scientific image, licensed; tiny type; strong negative space. Very effective for culture brands and album and podcast art.
- Related: `visual-styles` → `anti-design-ugly.md`, `retro-print-risograph.md`, `kinetic-typography.md`.

**Pitfalls**
- **Image rights.** *Unknown Pleasures* used a published scientific plot [16], and Reid used a Beaton photograph [14]. In commercial work, license or create the source.
- **Punk as costume.** Corporate brands using ransom-note type for a sale read as fake. Use it only when the content is actually oppositional or grassroots.
- **Symbols.** Reid's swastikas were provocation in 1977 context. Never reuse hate symbols as "edge."
- **Attribution disputes.** Note contested authorship when citing (e.g., "Nowhere Buses" [14]).

**Legacy**
- *Unknown Pleasures* is one of the most reproduced album images.
- Saville's appropriation method anticipates moodboard-driven brand design.
- Greiman's work is an early model of computer-native graphic design.

## Grunge / "Deconstruction" (mid-1980s–late 1990s; Berkeley, Cranbrook, California)

**Context: why it happened**
- **Tools:** *Emigre* (Berkeley, 1984–2005) was among the first publications designed on Macintosh computers. Zuzana Licko's bitmap typefaces appeared from 1985, and Emigre Fonts licensed digital fonts by 1986 [9].
- **Theory:** "deconstruction" came from Jacques Derrida's *Of Grammatology* (France 1967; English 1976). It reached designers through literary studies and Cranbrook [7].
- **Exhibition:** MoMA's 1988 *Deconstructivist Architecture* (Philip Johnson and Mark Wigley) gave the term a look of fragmented, twisted forms. Designers quickly adopted it [7].
- **Industry:** Desktop publishing let small teams produce magazines (Carson's *Beach Culture* from 1989 [11]). Youth and music markets rewarded visual noise.

**Key figures and works**

| Person / group | Work | Year | Significance |
|----------------|------|------|--------------|
| Rudy VanderLans (editor/designer) and Zuzana Licko (typefaces) | *Emigre* magazine, 69 issues | 1984–2005 | A forum for the "new typography" and the legibility debate. MoMA exhibited all 69 issues in 2007 [9]. |
| Zuzana Licko | Bitmap fonts for early Macintosh | From 1985 | Type designed for the screen's constraints. Her line "You read best what you read most" sparked the "Legibility Wars" [9]. |
| Cranbrook students and alumni (Edward Fella, Jeffery Keedy, Allen Hori) | *Output* (student publication); Keedy's *Lushus* typeface; Fella's announcements | 1980s–1992 | The experimental core that Heller criticized in 1993 [8]. |
| Lorraine Wild | "Transgression and Delight: Graphic Design at Cranbrook" (essay) | 1990 | Contemporary account of the Cranbrook program [8]. |
| David Carson (b. 1955) | *Transworld Skateboarding*; *Beach Culture* (art director from 1989; six issues); *Ray Gun* (three years, early–mid 1990s) | 1980s–1990s | Layered, collage-like covers and "dirty" photographic technique [11]. |
| David Carson | *The End of Print* (Chronicle Books) | 1995 | Monograph whose title named the moment [11]. |
| Ellen Lupton and J. Abbott Miller | "Deconstruction and Graphic Design: History Meets Theory," *Visible Language* 28(4) | 1 October 1994 | Argues deconstruction is a critical method, not a style, and proposes a typographic history informed by it [6]. |

**The debates (present both sides)**
- **Vignelli vs. Emigre (1991).**
  - Massimo Vignelli criticized *Emigre* at a typography panel reported in *Print* in 1991. Secondary sources give the wording as "garbage" [9] or "factory of garbage" [10]. "An aberration of culture" is attributed in some accounts [unverified]. The original *Print* transcript was not opened in this pass, so quote it as reported, not verbatim.
  - The criticism raised *Emigre*'s profile [9].
  - Vignelli's broader position appears in a 1991 poster reproduced in *The Vignelli Canon*. There he wrote that "the proliferation of typefaces and type manipulations represents a new level of visual pollution" and that "all we need are a few basic ones" [17].
- **Heller, "The Cult of the Ugly" (*Eye* no. 9, Summer 1993).**
  - Steven Heller argued that experimentation needs discipline: "the engine floods when too much instinct and not enough intelligence or discipline is injected into the mix" [8].
  - His targets were Cranbrook's *Output*, Keedy's *Lushus*, and Fella's announcements [8].
  - The article records VanderLans's response to Vignelli [8].
- **The counter-argument.**
  - Licko: legibility is learned and habitual ("You read best what you read most") [9].
  - McCoy: "Post-structuralism is an attitude, not a style" (quoted by Lupton) [7].
- **Fair summary:** both sides were partly right. Unfamiliar letterforms can become readable with exposure, which is Licko's point. But layered type over images costs readers time, which is the modernists' point.
- **Readability effects:** treat claims about readability effects as **[E2]/[E3]** unless tested. Test your own layouts with real readers.

**Manifestos and key texts (dated)**
- 1966 / 1976 English: Derrida, *Of Grammatology* (via [7]).
- 1984–2005: *Emigre* [9].
- 1990: Wild, "Transgression and Delight" [8].
- 1991: Vignelli in *Print* (as reported) [9][10]; *Cranbrook Design: The New Discourse* [5].
- 1993: Heller, "The Cult of the Ugly" [8].
- 1994: Lupton and Miller, *Visible Language* [6].
- 1995: Carson, *The End of Print* [11].

**Visual DNA**
- **Type:** bitmap and low-resolution faces; hybrid and distorted letterforms; overlapping text; tight negative leading; text as texture.
- **Color:** often muted or photographic; heavy black; occasional acid accents.
- **Form and layout:** no visible grid; fragmented columns; type running into gutters and images.
- **Imagery:** blurred, cropped, high-grain, or photocopied photography [11].
- **Production technology:** Macintosh, PostScript, QuarkXPress-era desktop publishing; scanning and photocopying.

**Apply today**
- Use for music, skate, streetwear, zines, and art festivals, or for a single campaign moment that must feel raw.
- **Tokens:**
  - Newsprint `#EDEAE2` with ink `#111111` (15.71:1) [45].
  - Overlap text layers with `mix-blend-mode: multiply`.
  - Negative tracking (−0.04em) on display text.
  - Real scan textures.
- Keep the reading path (nav, price, form fields) in a clean layer.
- **Typefaces [44]:**
  - Darker Grotesque and Syne (Google Fonts) for distorted-display feel.
  - Space Mono or VT323 for bitmap and terminal echoes. VT323 is built from DEC VT320 terminal glyphs.
- Related: `visual-styles` → `anti-design-ugly.md`, `brutalism-web.md`, `maximalism.md`.

**Pitfalls**
- **Style label instead of method.** Lupton: "'Deconstruction', 'deconstructivism', and just plain 'decon' became design-world clichés" [7].
- **Accessibility.** Overlapping type, low contrast, and text in images fail screen readers and contrast rules. Provide a real-text, accessible layer.
- **Myth: "Emigre was just chaos."** It was also a type foundry and a critical journal [9].

**Legacy**
- Emigre's model of a designer-run foundry plus publication foreshadows independent type foundries and design newsletters.
- The legibility debate returns with every new aesthetic (brutalism, neo-brutalism, Liquid Glass).

## Digital eras (1990–present)

### Early web (1990–c.2000)

| Date | Milestone | Source |
|------|-----------|--------|
| 20 December 1990 | First website published at CERN (info.cern.ch), describing the project. | [18] |
| 6 August 1991 | Tim Berners-Lee posts the project summary to the alt.hypertext newsgroup and invites collaborators. | [18] |
| 30 April 1993 | CERN releases the Web into the public domain, free to anyone. | [18] |
| Mid-1990s | 216-color "web-safe" palette (6×6×6; channel values 00, 33, 66, 99, CC, FF) for 256-color displays. | [19] |
| 1996 | David Siegel publishes the single-pixel spacer-GIF trick in *Creating Killer Web Sites*. | [20] |
| 1996 | Matthew Carter's Verdana released by Microsoft for small-size screen legibility (hinting by Tom Rickner). Distributed free via Core fonts for the Web until 2002. | [21] |

- **Visual DNA:** table layouts held together by transparent spacer GIFs [20]; web-safe color; system and core fonts; gray default backgrounds; blue underlined links; tiled backgrounds; GIF animation.
- **Why it ended:** CSS let developers set sizes, margins, and padding directly, making spacers unnecessary. Later, fluid layouts replaced fixed tables [20]. With 24-bit displays in the 2000s, web-safe colors stopped mattering [19].
- **Apply today:**
  - Retro-tech references only. Use web-safe values for authenticity: `#000099` on `#FFFFCC` (13.99:1); `#336699` with white text (6.0:1) [45].
  - Build with CSS grid, never tables.
  - Related: `visual-styles` → `y2k.md`, `brutalism-web.md`.

### Web 2.0 and the glossy era (2004–c.2013)

| Date | Milestone | Source |
|------|-----------|--------|
| January 1999 | Darcy DiNucci coins "Web 2.0" in "Fragmented Future." | [22] |
| 2004 | Tim O'Reilly and Dale Dougherty popularize the term at the first Web 2.0 Conference. | [22] |
| 2006 | Windows Vista: "one of the first mature examples" of the glossy "Frutiger Aero" look. | [23] |
| 2018 | Sofi Xian (Consumer Aesthetics Research Institute) names "Frutiger Aero" retrospectively. Most critics place the aesthetic at about 2004–2013. | [23] |

- **Visual DNA:** glossy textures, reflections, 3D graphics, bright colors, skeuomorphic touches [23]. The reflective look was also called "Web 2.0 Gloss" [23].
- **Commonly cited (craft description, not sourced here):** rounded-corner buttons, vertical gradients, wet-floor reflections, starburst "beta" badges, large friendly type.
- **Note on terms:** "Web 2.0" is a business and technology term (user-generated content, interactive web apps), not a style name. Berners-Lee faulted it for lacking coherent meaning [22]. "Frutiger Aero" is a 2018 retrospective label [23].
- **Accessibility:** dynamic content and visual effects made accessibility harder [22].
- **Apply today:** nostalgia campaigns only. See `visual-styles` → `y2k.md`, `skeuomorphism.md`, `gradient-aurora-mesh.md` (its modern gradient descendant).

### Skeuomorphism to flat (2010–2014)

| Date | Milestone | Source |
|------|-----------|--------|
| 2010 | Windows Phone 7 formally introduces Metro, a language based on Swiss graphic design and transit signage, with Segoe type and "content before chrome." Zune's interface preceded it (refreshed 2007). | [26] |
| 2012 | Microsoft drops the "Metro" name in August amid trademark concerns. "Microsoft design language" becomes official in September. | [26] |
| October 2012 | Scott Forstall, Apple's most senior proponent of skeuomorphic style, leaves; Jony Ive takes over some of his responsibilities. | [24] |
| 10 June 2013 | Apple unveils iOS 7: translucency, layers, refined type, clutter and ornamentation removed. Ive: "There is a profound and enduring beauty in simplicity, in clarity, in efficiency." | [25][24] |
| 25 June 2014 | Google announces Material Design (codename "Quantum Paper") at I/O. Paper-and-ink metaphor with shadows for depth. Head designer Matías Duarte. | [28][27] |
| 2017 | Microsoft Fluent Design System adds depth and "Acrylic" translucency, gradually replacing Metro. | [26][27] |

- **The usability argument:**
  - Nielsen called flat design a "threat to tablet usability" in 2013 [27].
  - NN/g's 2017 eyetracking study (71 users, 9 page pairs) found weak-signifier, flat-styled versions took about 22% longer and drew about 25% more fixations to find the click target. There was no difference on simple, conventional, high-contrast pages [29]. **[E2]**
  - The study measured target-finding only, not whole tasks [29].
- **Apply today:**
  - Flat is the baseline, but keep signifiers: underlined or colored links, button shapes, focus rings.
  - See `visual-styles` → `flat-design.md`, `material-design.md`, `skeuomorphism.md`, `swiss-international.md`.

### Responsive web (2010–)

| Date | Milestone | Source |
|------|-----------|--------|
| 25 May 2010 | Ethan Marcotte, "Responsive Web Design," *A List Apart*: fluid grids, flexible images, media queries. Inspired by "responsive architecture." | [30] |

- **Apply:** design content-first at the smallest width; fluid type with `clamp()`; container queries for components. Every era after 2010 assumes this.

| 10 June 2015 | IBM Carbon Design System: public development begins; open source (Apache 2.0); implements the IBM Design Language. | [32] |
| 2017 | Shopify introduces Polaris at Unite 2017 to "help unify the look and feel of all parts of the Shopify ecosystem." | [33] |
| May 2021 | Material You / Material Design 3: themes generated from the user's wallpaper (Android 12). | [28] |
| May 2025 | Material 3 Expressive (Android 16, Wear OS 6). | [28] |
| 28 October 2025 | W3C Design Tokens Community Group (DTCG) announces the first stable Format Specification (2025.10). Adopters include Adobe, Figma, Sketch, Penpot, Style Dictionary, Tokens Studio. | [34] |

- **Lineage:** this is the digital continuation of the Unimark and NYCTA manual tradition (`movements-1945-1979.md`): rules over artifacts.
- **Apply:**
  - Express tokens in DTCG format (JSON with `$value` and `$type`) so they move between Figma and code.
  - Layer them: primitive, then semantic, then component.
  - Document "why," not only "what."
- **Pitfall:** systems flatten brand expression. Every 2020s style below is partly a reaction to that sameness.

**Neo-brutalism**
- **What it is:** high contrast, blocky layouts, bold colors, thick borders, solid offset shadows, and quirky type with generous whitespace. It is more colorful and orderly than raw web brutalism [38].
- **Origin:** NN/g gives no dated origin [38]. Write "early 2020s" without a precise first instance [exact origin unverified].
- **Apply:**
  - `border: 3px solid #000; box-shadow: 4px 4px 0 #000;`
  - Fills such as `#FFDE59` with black text (15.84:1), `#FF90E8` with black text (10.41:1), `#C9F35B` with black text (16.45:1) [45].
  - Limit the palette to two or three bold colors, and check contrast. NN/g warns that pairings like yellow and cyan can fail [38].
  - See `visual-styles` → `neo-brutalism.md`, `brutalism-web.md`.
- **Lineage:** Pop's outlines and flat process color (`movements-1945-1979.md`) plus the early web's raw HTML.

**Glassmorphism to Liquid Glass**
- **Lineage:** Aero (2006–2009) → iOS 7 translucency (2013) → Fluent Acrylic (2017) → "glassmorphism" (2020) → Liquid Glass (2025) [23][25][26][37][39].
- **Apply:** glass on controls and navigation only, never under body text. Provide reduced-transparency fallbacks. NN/g documents legibility and target-size failures in iOS 26 [40] **[E2]**.
- See `visual-styles` → `glassmorphism.md`, `apple-style.md`.

**Variable fonts**
- **Since September 2016**, one font file can carry continuous axes: weight, width, optical size, and custom axes [35][36].
- **Apply:**
  - Use one variable family for the whole type system to cut font payload.
  - Animate `font-variation-settings` for kinetic type.
  - Use optical size (`opsz`) for display vs. text.
- **Verified free variable families (Google Fonts) [44]:**
  - Inter (opsz, wght).
  - Roboto (wdth, wght).
  - IBM Plex Sans (wdth, wght).
  - Bricolage Grotesque (opsz, wdth, wght).
  - Fraunces (SOFT, WONK, opsz, wght).
- See `visual-styles` → `kinetic-typography.md`.

**Generative AI imagery: design implications**
- **Timeline:** the dated milestones are in the table above. The shift from research demo (January 2021) to mass tools (mid-to-late 2022) took about 18 months [41][42][43].
- **Ownership:**
  - The US Copyright Office has issued registration guidance (March 2023) and a copyrightability report (January 2025) [46].
  - This file did not open the report's text. Read Part 2 before advising on registrability of AI-assisted work. Do not summarize it from memory.
  - Treat this as a volatile, jurisdiction-specific fact (verified 2026-10).
- **Litigation risk:**
  - Training-data and output suits against Stability AI, Midjourney, and others have been active since January 2023 [42][43].
  - As of November 2025, Getty had largely lost its UK case against Stability AI [43].
  - Check vendor indemnities and training-data terms before using generated images in client work.
- **Practice rules:**
  - Disclose AI use to clients.
  - Do not prompt for a living artist's style or a named brand's IP.
  - Use generation for exploration, moodboards, and backgrounds. Commission or photograph hero assets when ownership matters.
  - Keep provenance records (tool, date, prompt, edits).
- **Aesthetic consequence:** generic "AI look" imagery (smooth gradients, hyper-detailed lighting, uncanny anatomy) is now recognizable. Distinctive brands lean on human-made texture, photography, and typography to differentiate. This is an observation **[E3]**, not measured.
- Related: `visual-styles` → `gradient-aurora-mesh.md`, `immersive-3d-webgl.md`, `anti-design-ugly.md`, `organic-biophilic.md`.

## Cross-cutting lessons

1. **The legibility argument repeats every decade.** Crouwel vs. van Toorn (1972), Vignelli vs. Emigre (1991), Heller vs. Cranbrook (1993), flat vs. signifiers (2013–2017), Liquid Glass (2025) [8][9][29][40]. Decide which text must be read fast, and protect it. Experiment everywhere else.
2. **Technology sets the look, then the look outlives the technology.** Bitmap fonts (1985), spacer GIFs (1996), web-safe colors (mid-1990s), gloss (2006), and GPU glass (2025) were constraints or capabilities first [9][19][20][23][39]. When reviving one, reproduce the constraint deliberately.
3. **Systems and their rebellions alternate.** Atomic Design, Carbon, Polaris, and DTCG standardized interfaces [31][32][33][34]. Neo-brutalism, glass, and anti-design push back. Build expressive layers *on top of* a token system, not instead of one.
4. **Labels arrive late and flatten meaning.** "Mid-century modern" (1984), "Frutiger Aero" (2018), and "deconstruction" as a style tag [7][23] are retrospective names. Use them for search, not as history.
5. **Platform makers now set aesthetics.** Metro (2010), iOS 7 (2013), Material (2014), and Liquid Glass (2025) moved the whole industry within months [25][26][28][39]. Track platform guidelines, and verify their dates.

## Common myths

| Myth | Correction |
|------|------------|
| "Vignelli said Emigre was 'a garbage.'" | Reported wordings are "garbage" [9] and "factory of garbage" [10], from a 1991 *Print* panel. The verbatim original was not verified in this pass. Quote as reported. |
| "Deconstruction is a visual style." | Lupton and Miller (1994) and Lupton (2004) argue it is a critical method that became a style label [6][7]. |
| "The web began in 1991." | The first site went online on 20 December 1990. The public newsgroup announcement was 6 August 1991 [18]. Say which milestone you mean. |
| "Web 2.0 is a visual style." | It is a business and technology term (DiNucci 1999; O'Reilly and Dougherty 2004) [22]. The glossy look is now called "Frutiger Aero" (named 2018) [23]. |
| "Apple invented flat UI with iOS 7." | Microsoft's Metro shipped with Windows Phone 7 in 2010 [26]. |
| "Material Design is flat design." | Material uses shadows and a paper metaphor for depth [27][28]. |
| "Memphis disbanded in 1988." | Sources disagree, 1987 [12] or 1988 [13]. Write c. 1987–88. |
| "Weingart founded a style." | "I never intended to create a 'style'" [2]. |
| "Variable fonts are new." | Apple's TrueType GX variations and Adobe's Multiple Masters preceded OpenType 1.8 (2016) [36]. |
| "Unknown Pleasures is an 1980s cover." | It was released June 1979 [16]. |
| "AI images are automatically free to use." | Ownership and training-data liability are unsettled and jurisdiction-specific [42][43][46]. Check before commercial use. |
