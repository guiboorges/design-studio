# Brand Strategy and Concept

> **Scope:** How to run the first three stages of a guided branding engagement with a non-designer (discovery conversation, strategic routes, one central concept), with an evidence-graded view of distinctiveness, differentiation, personality, archetypes, and consistency claims, plus naming basics and the brand core template every later deliverable reads from.
> **Last verified:** 2026-10 (volatile items: trademark search tools and URLs, studio case-study pages, TMview coverage figure dated 2022-01)
> **Used by:** brand-strategist, creative-director, identity-designer

Related skills. Masters, their logo principles, and the C&G&H, Bierut, and Mastercard precedents live in
`design-history` (`masters-graphic-contemporary.md`). Biases, persuasion, audience, and culture live in
`cognitive-psychology` (`cognitive-biases.md`, `persuasion-and-behavior.md`, `audience-and-culture.md`).
The visual territory that follows the concept stage draws on `visual-styles`. This file does not repeat them.

**Gate rule.** Do not start a stage until the user has approved the previous stage's output in writing
("yes, route B", "approved"). Record the approval and date in the brand core decisions log (§6). This
mirrors the phase structure professional texts use. Wheeler and Meyerson's *Designing Brand Identity*
(6th ed., 2024) organizes its process into five phases: conducting research, clarifying strategy, designing
identity, creating touchpoints, and managing assets [1]. Our stages 1–3 sit inside its first two phases.

## 1. Discovery

### 1.1 What professional studios and authors actually do at kickoff

| Source | What it contributes to discovery | Evidence type |
|---|---|---|
| Wheeler & Meyerson, *Designing Brand Identity* 6th ed. (Wiley, 2024) | Research and strategy come before design. Five named phases [1]. | Practitioner text [E2] |
| GV "three-hour brand sprint" (Jake Knapp, summarized by Reforge, 2019) | Six timed exercises run with 2–6 executives and a "decider": 20-year roadmap, What/How/Why, top 3 values, top 3 audiences, personality sliders, competitive 2×2 [2]. | Practitioner method [E2]; secondary summary |
| April Dunford, positioning (2019 book; 2021 quickstart post) | Start from competitive alternatives ("what would customers do if our solution didn't exist"), then unique attributes, value, target customers, market category [3]. | Practitioner method [E2] |
| Marty Neumeier, *Zag* (onlyness statement) | Force the brand to say what it is the "only" one of, in six slots: what, how, who, where, why, when [4][5]. | Practitioner method [E2]; via secondary source |
| Mozilla open design (johnson banks, 2016–17) | A public, gated process with explicit rules ("We won't vote on the outcome") and a published log of rejected routes [6][7]. | Documented case [E2] |
| Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv | Evaluation criteria for marks (appropriate, distinctive, flexible). See `design-history` → C&G&H. | Practitioner [E2] |

No major studio publishes its full client questionnaire. The question bank below is a synthesis of the
methods above, organized so a non-designer can answer it in conversation. Where a question comes from
a named method, the source is cited.

### 1.2 Conversation rules for guiding a non-designer

| Rule | How to do it |
|---|---|
| Ask in small batches | At most 3 questions per turn. Group by theme (§1.3). Start with the business, not the look. |
| Always offer a default | Every question has a suggested answer the user can accept ("If you're not sure, I'll assume X. Okay?"). Silence is not consent; ask for a yes. |
| Translate jargon | Say "who would you lose customers to?" instead of "competitive set"; "what you'd never want to be mistaken for" instead of "brand guardrails". |
| Reflect back | After each theme, restate the answers in one or two sentences and ask the user to correct them. |
| Separate facts from wishes | Tag answers as *fact* (we serve dentists in Lisbon), *belief* (customers choose us for speed), or *aspiration* (we want to feel premium). Beliefs need proof before they go into positioning. |
| Don't ask about visuals yet | Colors, logos, and fonts come after the concept. If the user volunteers likes and dislikes, log them as "early visual signals" and move on. |
| Watch for the curse of knowledge | Founders overrate how well outsiders know their category. The same bias shows in marketers' judgments of brand assets [13]. Ask "how would a first-time customer describe you?" |
| Stop when you can write the summary | Discovery is done when you can fill the discovery summary (§1.5) without inventing anything. Mark gaps as open questions rather than prolonging the interview. |

### 1.3 Question bank

Ask the **core** questions (marked C) in every engagement. Ask the others when the project needs them
(naming, rebrand, multi-market, etc.).

#### A. The business and the offer

| # | Question (plain words) | Why it matters | Default if "I don't know" |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 C | In one sentence, what do you sell and to whom? | Becomes the "What" in What/How/Why [2] and the descriptor line in the brand core. | Draft it from their website or pitch and ask them to edit. |
| A2 C | What problem does a customer have right before they find you? | The trigger moment that the story (§2.1) starts from. | "They're losing time or money on [task] and the current way is frustrating." Confirm. |
| A3 C | How do you do it differently from the usual way? | The "How" circle [2]; seeds unique attributes [3]. | List 3 operational differences you can see (price model, speed, method, channel). Ask which is real. |
| A4 C | Why does the company exist beyond making money? | The "Why" circle, described as "the reason you get out of bed in the morning" [2]. Basis for purpose. | Offer two candidate whys (one about customers, one about the craft). Let them pick or merge. |
| A5 | What are you proudest of that customers don't notice? | Often the raw material for the concept (§4.2, "extrapolate a characteristic"). | Skip; revisit after customer quotes. |
| A6 | What do you refuse to do, even if it would make money? | Values only mean something when they cost something. | "We won't [cut a corner common in the category]." Confirm. |
| A7 | What is the price position: cheaper, same, or more expensive than alternatives? | Constrains personality (Mass ↔ Elite slider [2]) and visual territory. | Assume "about the same" and flag as open. |

#### B. Audience

| # | Question | Why it matters | Default |
|---|---|---|---|
| B1 C | Who are your top 3 audiences, in order? Include non-buyers (hires, investors, partners, regulators). | GV's sprint ranks three audiences because a brand speaks to more than customers [2]. | 1. Primary buyer, 2. End user (if different), 3. Future hires. |
| B2 C | Describe your best customer: what they do, what they care about, what they already use. | Dunford's target customer is the one who "cares a lot about your differentiated value" [3]. | Ask for one real customer by first name and describe that person. |
| B3 | What words do customers use when they recommend you? | Customer language beats founder language for voice and proof. | Ask for 3 reviews, emails, or testimonials to read. |
| B4 | Who is *not* your customer? | Prevents an "everyone" brand. | "People who need [the premium or the bargain end] of the category." |
| B5 | Which markets and languages, now and in 3 years? | Naming, color meaning, script support, and trademark scope depend on it. See `cognitive-psychology` → `audience-and-culture.md`. | Current country + English. Flag for naming (§5). |
| B6 | Any audience needs we must design for (older users, low vision, low bandwidth, low literacy)? | Sets accessibility constraints early. | Assume WCAG 2.2 AA everywhere; ask about anything beyond. |

#### C. Competition and alternatives

| # | Question | Why it matters | Default |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 C | If you didn't exist, what would your customers do instead? | Dunford's first step: competitive alternatives include spreadsheets, agencies, or "do nothing", not only rival brands [3]. | List: direct rival, a manual workaround, a generalist tool, do nothing. Confirm. |
| C2 C | Name 2–4 brands you'd lose a deal to. | Inputs to the competitor map (§1.4) and the onlyness swap test [4]. | Search the category and propose 4; ask which matter. |
| C3 | What do all of them look and sound like? | The category's visual and verbal conventions: what to break or keep (§2.3). | Agent audits their sites and summarizes the shared conventions. |
| C4 | What do you have that none of the alternatives have? | Unique attributes, which Dunford keeps as features before translating to value [3]. | Pull from A3; ask for proof of each. |
| C5 | Which competitor brand do you secretly admire, and why? | Reveals aspiration and taste without asking about visuals. | Skip. |

#### D. Ambition and future

| # | Question | Why it matters | Default |
|---|---|---|---|
| D1 C | Where will the company be in 5, 10, 15, and 20 years? | GV's 20-year roadmap keeps the brand from being built only for today's product [2]. | 5 years: the current product, larger. 10: adjacent products. Mark 15–20 as open. |
| D2 | What would make this rebrand a success one year from now? | Defines measures (§6). | "Customers and hires describe us the way the brand core says." |
| D3 | Is a bigger change coming (new category, merger, IPO, AI pivot)? | Uber's identity was briefed around a shift "from app to mobility company" [21]; Stack Overflow's around AI changing its role [22]. | Assume none; ask again at the strategy stage. |

#### E. Personality, values, voice

| # | Question | Why it matters | Default |
|---|---|---|---|
| E1 C | Pick your top 3 values and rank them. | GV's sprint narrows values to three, ranked, with the top one "most important" [2]. | Offer 8 candidates derived from A4–A6; ask them to rank 3. |
| E2 C | Place the brand on four sliders: Friend ↔ Authority, Young & Innovative ↔ Mature & Classic, Playful ↔ Serious, Mass Appeal ↔ Elite. | GV's personality sliders; the advice is to be strongly opinionated on one or two [2]. | Run the slider exercise (§1.4) instead of asking abstractly. |
| E3 | If the brand were a person at a dinner party, how would they behave? | Projective prompt; useful for voice. Treat output as creative input, not measurement (§3.3). | Offer two contrasting descriptions and ask which is closer. |
| E4 | Which words should never describe the brand? | Defines the "is not" column of personality. | Propose the opposite end of their strongest slider. |

#### F. Equity, constraints, practicalities

| # | Question | Why it matters | Default |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 C | Is this a new brand or a change to an existing one? What must we keep? | Existing recognizable elements are assets. Pentagram kept Slack's octothorpe "to retain the equity" [17]; Further kept Deliveroo's kangaroo [25]. | For a rebrand: list current name, color, symbol, sounds, phrases. Keep anything customers recognize until §3.2 says otherwise. |
| F2 C | Is the name fixed? | If not, naming runs in parallel (§5). | Assume fixed. |
| F3 C | Where will the brand appear first? (website, app, social, packaging, signage) | Order of applications; sets size and motion constraints for the logo. | Website + social + app icon/favicon. |
| F4 | Budget, deadline, and who decides? | GV insists on a single "decider" [2]. | Decider = founder or CEO. Ask who else must be consulted. |
| F5 | Any legal, regulatory, or cultural limits? (health claims, finance rules, religious or political sensitivities) | Some routes are off-limits in regulated categories. | Assume general consumer law; flag regulated categories. |

### 1.4 Workshop exercises the agent can run in chat

| Exercise | Source | How to run it in conversation | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20-year roadmap | GV brand sprint [2] | Ask for one line per milestone (5, 10, 15, 20 years). Accept blanks after 10. | 4 lines in the brand core "ambition" field. |
| What / How / Why | GV brand sprint [2] | Three concentric circles: What (outer), How (middle), Why (inner). Collect several answers per circle, then make the user choose one each. | 3 sentences. |
| Top 3 values | GV brand sprint [2] | Offer 8–12 candidates. Ask the user to cross out until 3 remain, then rank. For each, ask "what behavior proves it?" | 3 ranked values with a behavior each. |
| Top 3 audiences | GV brand sprint [2] | List all audiences, including employees, press, regulators. Rank 3. | Ranked list. |
| Personality sliders | GV brand sprint [2] | Show each slider as a 1–5 scale with a known brand at each end to anchor it (choose brands from the user's market). Ask for a number. Push for at least one extreme (1 or 5). | 4 numbers + 1–2 "strongly opinionated" sliders. |
| This-or-that pairs | Adaptation of sliders | Rapid binary choices on words, not visuals: "quiet or loud", "expert or companion", "precise or warm", "invisible or on display". Ten pairs, answered fast. Disagreement between this and the sliders is a discussion point. | Tension list feeding §2.3. |
| Attribute cards | Trait vocabulary from Aaker's brand personality scale (42 traits, five dimensions) [14] | Present 15–20 adjectives (sincere, daring, reliable, sophisticated, tough…). User keeps 5, then ranks 3. Use it as a vocabulary, not as a measurement (§3.3). | 3–5 personality adjectives + "is not" list. |
| "If the brand were…" | Projective technique; no validated source opened [unverified] | "If the brand were a car / a city / a material / a sound, which one, and why?" Only the *why* is useful. | Raw metaphors for §4. |
| Competitor map (2×2) | GV brand sprint, axes Classic ↔ Modern and Expressive ↔ Reserved [2] | Plot 4–6 alternatives from C1–C2, then the brand where it is today and where it wants to be. Swap axes if these don't separate the field (e.g., Human ↔ Automated, Specialist ↔ Generalist). | A map showing white space; feeds route design. |
| Onlyness statement + swap test | Neumeier, *Zag*, via Mall [4] | Fill what, how, who, where, why, when. Then put a competitor's name in. If it still fits, the statement is not distinctive [4]. | One sentence that fails for competitors. |
| Customer-voice audit | Practice | Read 10+ reviews or support emails. Extract repeated words and the main before/after. | Voice evidence; proof points. |
| Category convention audit | Practice | Screenshot 5 competitor homepages. List shared color families, type styles, imagery, and claims. | "Conventions to keep / to break" list for §2.3. |

**Apply.** In chat, run sliders and this-or-that pairs first. They are fast, they feel like a game,
and they expose disagreement early. Save the roadmap and values for when the user is engaged.
**Limit.** These exercises are elicitation tools. They organize opinions. They do not show how
customers perceive the brand. Label their outputs "internal view" until customer evidence exists.

## 2. Strategy

### 2.1 Brand platform components

Write each component so it passes its test. Examples are for the fictional company in §6 unless a
source is cited.

| Component | Definition | Test | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Why the company exists beyond profit. The "Why" circle [2]. | Would a competitor say the same? Would employees feel it is true? | "Give small clinics their time back." (fictional) |
| **Vision** | The world or company state you are working toward, usually 10+ years out. | Is it a future state, not a task? Does it outlast the current product? | "Every clinic runs on time, without a full-time scheduler." (fictional) |
| **Mission** | What the company does now, for whom, to move toward the vision. | Is it concrete enough to say no to a project? | "We automate scheduling and reminders for independent clinics in Europe." (fictional) |
| **Positioning** | The place the brand occupies in a target customer's mind relative to the alternatives. "The mental perception of a product or brand by customers" [8]. | Can it be written in a template (§2.2) and fail the competitor swap test? | See §6. |
| **Value proposition** | The specific benefit a target customer gets, backed by proof. Dunford derives value from attributes by asking "So what for customers?" [3]. | Is it a benefit (what the user can do) rather than a feature? Is there proof? | "Fill cancelled slots automatically, so the day stays full." (fictional) |
| **Personality** | The human traits associated with the brand. Aaker's formal definition: "the set of human characteristics associated with a brand" [14]. | Expressed as slider positions + 3 adjectives + "is not". | Calm, precise, quietly warm; not cute, not clinical. (fictional) |
| **Voice** | How the personality sounds in words: 3–4 principles with do/don't examples. | Could a new writer apply it to an error message and a billboard? | "Say the time, not the feeling: 'Your 3 pm is filled.'" (fictional) |
| **Story** | The narrative arc: before (the problem), the turn (what the brand does), after (the changed state). | Does the customer, not the company, change? | Before: the phone rings all day. Turn: the system handles it. After: the front desk is quiet. (fictional) |
| **Values** | 3 ranked principles that guide decisions, each with a behavior. | Does each one cost something? | 1. Punctual. 2. Plain. 3. Discreet. (fictional) |

Real-world reference for purpose and mission language: Further (formerly DesignStudio) defined Airbnb's
brand mission as "Belong Anywhere", which became the reason behind the company's decisions [16].

### 2.2 Positioning methods

| Method | Origin | Core move | Template or steps | Use when | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ries & Trout positioning** | Articles in *Industrial Marketing* (1969) and *Advertising Age* (1972); book *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981) [8] | Positioning happens in the customer's mind; it is "an organized system for finding a window in the mind" [8]. Find an open slot or reframe a rival. | No fixed template. Ask: what position do we own now, what do we want, whom must we outgun? | You need a sharp competitive frame, especially in crowded categories. | [E2] Practitioner. Scholars dispute that Ries and Trout originated the concept; some credit Ogilvy in the 1950s [8]. |
| **Moore positioning statement** | Geoffrey Moore, *Crossing the Chasm* (1991 [year not on cited page]) [6] | A one-sentence internal statement linking customer, need, category, benefit, alternative, difference. | For (target customer) who (need or opportunity), the (product name) is a (product category) that (key benefit). Unlike (primary competitive alternative), our product (primary differentiation) [6]. | Tech and B2B products; aligning a team quickly. | [E2] Template; book wording not verified in this pass (taken from a secondary source [6]). |
| **Dunford, *Obviously Awesome*** | April Dunford, 2019 book; 2021 quickstart [3] | Positioning is built from five components in order: competitive alternatives, differentiated capabilities, value, target customers, market category [3]. | 1. Alternatives ("what would customers do if our solution didn't exist"). 2. Unique attributes ("what do we have that the alternatives do not?"). 3. Value ("so what for customers?"). 4. Best-fit customers. 5. Market category: the context in which "our value is obvious" [3]. | New categories, products customers misunderstand, B2B software. | [E2] |
| **Neumeier onlyness** | *Zag* [4][5] | "Onlyness is my term for a point of differentiation" [5]. Say what you are the only one of. | What / How / Who / Where / Why / When, then the competitor swap test [4]. Neumeier: onlyness "doesn't last forever. It's situational" [5]. | Small brands; founders who resist abstract frameworks. | [E2] |

**Procedure for the agent.**
1. Run Dunford's five steps on the discovery summary. This is the most concrete method for a non-designer.
2. Write the Moore sentence from the result. It is the internal positioning statement in the brand core.
3. Write the onlyness sentence and run the swap test against the 2–4 alternatives from C2.
4. Use Ries & Trout as a check: "What slot in the customer's mind does this claim? Is that slot free?"
5. If two methods give different market categories, that difference is a strategic route (§2.3), not an error.

**Limit.** Positioning frameworks are practitioner tools [E2]. None has controlled evidence that it
improves outcomes. Also read §3.1: buyers often do not perceive brands as very different, so positioning
should guide the company's choices and messages. It does not guarantee customers will notice a difference.

### 2.3 Presenting 2–3 strategic routes as genuine alternatives

A route is a coherent strategic bet: a frame of reference, an audience emphasis, and a personality,
with a concept seed. Routes must differ in **what they give up**, not just in wording.

**How to build routes**
1. Pick one or two **tensions** from discovery (sliders where the team disagreed, this-or-that pairs, the
   competitor map's white space, a gap between the market category options).
2. Build each route by taking one pole of the tension seriously. Write it so a reasonable founder could
   choose it.
3. Make each route cost something: a lost audience, a convention abandoned, a claim you can no longer make.
4. Give every route the same template (below) so the user compares like with like.
5. Recommend one, say why, and say what would make you change your mind.

**Tension pairs that commonly produce real routes**

| Tension | Pole A | Pole B | Typical fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visibility of technology | Technology that disappears (calm, invisible, outcomes first) | Technology on display (the machine, the data, the power is the hero) | A: consumer, health, home. B: developer tools, infrastructure, performance products. |
| Relationship | Friend (peer, companion) | Authority (expert, standard-setter) | GV slider [2] |
| Era | Young & Innovative | Mature & Classic | GV slider [2] |
| Tone | Playful | Serious | GV slider [2] |
| Reach | Mass appeal | Elite | GV slider [2] |
| Category stance | Leader of the existing category | Creator of a new category (Dunford's market-category choice [3]) | New category costs education budget. |
| Source of credibility | Human craft and judgment | Machine precision and scale | AI products: decide whether people or the system are the hero. Koto positioned Copilot content around people, "not the product" [26]. |
| Scope of belonging | Global, neutral, universal | Local, rooted, specific | Multi-market vs. home-market brands. |
| Openness | Authored, controlled | Community-made, open | Mozilla chose an open, gated process [6]. |
| Energy | Calm, steady | Energetic, kinetic | Affects motion and sound later. |
| Promise | Utility (it works) | Aspiration (who you become) | Utility for tools, aspiration for lifestyle. |
| Convention | Follow category codes (trust, familiarity) | Break category codes (salience) | Coinbase's work used a friendly giant to make crypto feel approachable rather than technical [23]. |

**Route card (use the same fields for each route)**

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Route name | Two or three words (e.g., "The Quiet Desk"). |
| Strategic bet | One sentence: "We win by…" |
| Frame of reference | Market category and the main alternative it is set against. |
| Audience emphasis | Which of the top 3 audiences leads. |
| Personality | Slider numbers; 3 adjectives. |
| Positioning sentence | Moore template filled in. |
| Concept seed | A first metaphor or image (not final; §4 refines it). |
| What it gains | Concrete upside. |
| What it gives up | Concrete cost. Required. |
| Risk and proof needed | What must be true; how to check. |
| Early cues | 3–5 words for later visual territory (no images yet). |

**Rules for presenting routes**

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Present 2 or 3 routes, never more. | Present a "straw man" route designed to lose. |
| State a recommendation and its reason. | Merge two routes into a compromise. Mozilla's team called a simple merger of two finalists "A boardroom compromise to please no-one" [7]. |
| Show each route's cost explicitly. | Let the user pick on wording or a visual mockup at this stage. |
| Allow combining only if the combination is a new, coherent bet with its own cost. | Restart discovery because the user dislikes all routes. First ask which part of each route felt wrong. |
| Log every rejected route and why in "roads not taken". Mozilla published its own [7]. | Delete rejected routes. They are useful when later work drifts. |

## 3. Evidence: what is known about distinctiveness, differentiation, personality, and consistency

### 3.1 Distinctiveness vs. differentiation

| Claim | What the evidence says | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Most buyers do not see the brands they buy as very different from rivals. | Ehrenberg-Bass Institute work (Romaniuk, Sharp, and Ehrenberg, "Evidence concerning the importance of perceived brand differentiation") reports that "most consumers do not perceive the brands they buy as significantly different from the competition", across categories and countries [9]. An independent dataset agrees: in Millward Brown's BrandZ data (over 6,000 brands, 10 years), among people who found a brand acceptable, an average of 18% agreed it was different from others; the range was 0–86% [10]. | [E1] for the descriptive finding (replicated across independent datasets) |
| Brands with similar attributes share buyers. | Ehrenberg-Bass reports that brands share customers with competitors (e.g., McDonald's and Burger King) rather than owning separate segments [9]. | [E1] for the pattern (Ehrenberg-Bass empirical laws) |
| Small brands are bought by fewer people, who are also slightly less loyal ("Double Jeopardy"). | Romaniuk cites the Law of Double Jeopardy and argues that efforts to create more loyal buyers "do not enhance brand development" [12]. | [E1] for Double Jeopardy as a pattern; [E2] for the strategic advice |
| "Differentiation doesn't matter." | Overstated. The researchers do not claim differentiation never exists; they challenge how large and decisive perceived differentiation is as the main driver of purchase [9]. Product and service differences still matter for whether a brand is acceptable at all. | [E3] as stated |
| Distinctiveness (being easily recognized and linked to the brand) matters for growth. | Ehrenberg-Bass argues for building memory structures through distinctive assets (colors, logos, characters) and mental availability. This is the institute's framework, supported by its own measurement work. Sharp's *How Brands Grow* (2010) is the main popular source [book not opened in this pass]. | [E2] |

**Apply.** In strategy, use positioning (§2.2) to make choices and messages coherent. In identity, design
for **distinctiveness**: a small set of assets that only this brand uses, used consistently. Do not promise
the client that a differentiated claim will, by itself, make customers see the brand as unique.
**Limit.** The Ehrenberg-Bass findings come mostly from repeat-purchase consumer categories and large
brands. For a new B2B tool or a category creator, differentiation is how you get considered at all. Treat
the "distinctiveness over differentiation" slogan as a correction to over-reliance on USPs, not a reason
to skip positioning.

### 3.2 Distinctive brand assets (Romaniuk)

Jenni Romaniuk's *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* (2018) formalizes the idea [book not opened in this pass].
Definition from Ehrenberg-Bass: "Distinctive assets are non-brand name elements, such as colours, logos
or characters that uniquely signal the brand" [11].

| Dimension | Meaning | Measured by |
|---|---|---|
| **Fame** | How many category buyers link the asset to the brand. | Show the asset without the name to category buyers; count the share who name the brand [11]. |
| **Uniqueness** | How exclusively the asset evokes this brand rather than competitors. Romaniuk's article calls uniqueness "most important" [11]. | Share of brand mentions that go to this brand vs. others [11]. |

**The Distinctive Asset Grid** [11]

| Quadrant | Fame | Uniqueness | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Use or Lose | High | High | Use everywhere; can stand in for the name. |
| Invest | Low | High | Build fame through consistent use. |
| Avoid | High | Low | Shared with competitors; it will point people to rivals. |
| Test or ignore | Low | Low | New or unproven; test, then revise or drop. |

**Marketers misjudge their own assets.** A 2025 *Journal of Brand Management* study (Brus, Hartnett,
Faulkner, Driesener; over 400 brand elements) found marketers' intuitive judgments were "rarely accurate":
they overestimated fame and underestimated uniqueness, likely "projecting their own familiarity" onto
consumers [13]. **[E2]** (one peer-reviewed study, consistent with the curse-of-knowledge bias in
`cognitive-psychology` → `cognitive-biases.md`).

**Apply.**
- In discovery (F1), list current assets and ask the user to rate fame and uniqueness, then say plainly
  that these guesses are usually wrong [13]. Where budget allows, test with 20–50 category buyers
  (show the asset without the name, ask which brand it is).
- In the concept stage, plan 1–3 assets the concept can own (a color pair, a shape, a motion behavior, a
  character, a sound). Deliberately avoid category codes that sit in the Avoid quadrant.
- For a rebrand, keep any Use-or-Lose asset unless there is a strong reason. Slack kept its octothorpe;
  Deliveroo kept its kangaroo (§4.4).

**Limit.** A new brand has no fame. Its assets all start in "Invest" or "Test". Uniqueness is the part a
designer controls at launch.

### 3.3 Brand personality (Aaker) and its critique

| Item | Evidence | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Aaker's five dimensions: Sincerity, Excitement, Competence, Sophistication, Ruggedness [14]. | *Journal of Marketing Research*, Aug 1997, 34, 347–356. 631 US respondents rated 37 brands on 114 traits; the final scale has 42 traits in 15 facets [14]. | [E1] as a US measurement scale from 1997 |
| The scale measures "brand personality". | Kapferer and Azoulay (2003) argue that current scales "do not in fact measure brand personality" and "merge a number of dimensions of brand identity -- personality being only one of them" [15]. | Contested |
| The dimensions are universal. | The original sample was American. Cross-cultural stability was not established by the 1997 paper [14]. | [E3] if claimed globally |

**Apply.** Use Aaker's trait list as a vocabulary for attribute cards (§1.4) and for writing the
personality field. Do not present slider scores or card picks as measurement.
**Limit.** Personality words are not a strategy. "Innovative, trustworthy, friendly" fits almost every
tech company. Force the "is not" column and the swap test.

### 3.4 Brand archetypes (Mark & Pearson, 2001)

*The Hero and the Outlaw: Building Extraordinary Brands Through the Power of Archetypes* (Margaret Mark and
Carol S. Pearson, McGraw-Hill, 2001) adapts Jungian ideas into 12 brand archetypes [book not opened in this
pass]. The commonly cited set is Innocent, Explorer, Sage, Hero, Outlaw, Magician, Regular Guy/Gal, Lover,
Jester, Caregiver, Creator, Ruler [unverified against the book].

| Question | Reading | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Is there evidence that matching an archetype improves brand performance? | No peer-reviewed validation was found in this research pass. The book's support is case-based. | [E3] |
| Is it useful? | As a shared vocabulary for tone and storytelling with non-designers, yes. It gives people a quick way to talk about character. | [E2] as a facilitation aid |
| Risks | Many brands in one category pick the same archetype (every security brand is "the Hero", every AI is "the Magician" or "the Sage"). That produces sameness, which works against distinctiveness (§3.2). | — |

**Apply.** Offer archetypes only if the user asks or is stuck on personality. Use them to generate the
"is not" list ("we are a Sage, not a Magician: we explain, we don't dazzle").
**Limit.** Never present an archetype as research-backed, and never let it replace the sliders and values.

### 3.5 "Consistency increases revenue by 23%"

| Step | Finding |
|---|---|
| How it circulates | Typically as "Research by Lucidpress found that consistent brand presentation across all platforms can increase revenue by up to 23%", with no link to the study [27]. |
| Claimed origin | Secondary sources attribute it to a Lucidpress (now Marq) and Demand Metric survey report, *The Impact of Brand Consistency*, from about 2016, with roughly 200 marketing respondents, and a later wave reporting 33% [unverified: original report not found or opened in this pass]. |
| What kind of number it is | A survey of marketers estimating the revenue they *attribute* to consistency. It is not a measured causal effect on revenue, and the respondents are the people whose work is being credited. |
| Grade | **[E3]**. Do not quote it to clients as a fact. |
| Corrected reading | There is a better-founded reason for consistency: distinctive assets only build fame through repeated, consistent use (§3.2) [11]. Say "consistency is how a brand's assets become recognizable" [E2], not "consistency adds 23% revenue". |

### 3.6 Evidence summary

| Topic | Grade | One-line rule |
|---|---|---|
| Low perceived differentiation of brands | E1 (descriptive) | Don't promise that a USP alone will make customers see the brand as unique. |
| Distinctive assets (fame × uniqueness) | E2 | Design 1–3 ownable assets; avoid category codes. |
| Marketers misjudge their assets | E2 | Test with real category buyers when possible. |
| Aaker personality dimensions | E1 (US scale) / contested construct | Use as vocabulary, not measurement. |
| Archetypes | E3 | Facilitation aid only. |
| Positioning frameworks (Ries & Trout, Moore, Dunford, Neumeier) | E2 | Use to make choices; they are not proven to cause outcomes. |
| "Consistency = +23% revenue" | E3 | Don't cite. Use the distinctive-asset argument instead. |
| Concept-led identities (§4) | E2 (documented practice) | Effects on recognition or sales are not established by these case studies [E3]. |

## 4. The central concept ("big idea")

### 4.1 What the concept is

The concept is the single idea that every deliverable expresses. The brand core stores it as:

| Part | Rule | Example (fictional, §6) |
|---|---|---|
| **Sentence** | One sentence, under 15 words, that says what the brand does for people in a way that implies a form. | "Ondal keeps the clinic's day in rhythm." |
| **Metaphor** | One concrete, drawable image from the subject's world or a neighboring one. | A metronome: a steady beat that keeps everyone in time. |
| **Three expressions** | How it shows in (1) the mark, (2) the system (layout, color, type, imagery), (3) motion or behavior. | Mark built on a single regular pulse; layouts on a strict beat grid; one global tempo for all motion. |

**Tests (all must pass before the user sees it)**

| Test | Question | Fail signal |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy fit | Does it express the chosen route's bet and personality? | It could belong to any of the routes. |
| Swap | Put a competitor's name in. Does it still work? | Yes. |
| Drawable | Can a non-designer sketch the metaphor in 10 seconds? Airbnb's symbol was meant to be "simple enough to be drawn by anyone" [16]. | It needs explaining. |
| Generative | Does it suggest at least three expressions (mark, system, motion)? | Only a logo idea. |
| Durable | Will it still be true at the 10-year roadmap line (D1)? | It describes a feature that will change. |
| Ownable | Can it lead to an asset in the "Invest" quadrant (unique) rather than a category code? | It produces the category's usual imagery (shields for security, brains or sparkles for AI). See `ai-slop`. |
| Truthful | Is it backed by something the company really does? | It requires a claim the product can't support. |

### 4.2 Methods to derive the concept

Generate at least 10 raw candidates across several methods, then filter with the tests above.

| Method | Prompt to the user or yourself | Documented example |
|---|---|---|
| **Metaphor in the name** | What image is already inside the name? | Windows: Pentagram took the name's original metaphor ("seeing into screens and systems") back to a window in perspective, asking "Your name is Windows. Why are you a flag?" [18] |
| **The most characteristic thing in the subject's world** | What symbol, syntax, tool, or gesture belongs only to this field? | Mozilla used the "://" of web addresses inside its name, a "nod to URL" language [6][28]. Stack Overflow made its symbol "look like building blocks in motion" and kept the toppling tower from the name's origin [22]. |
| **Extrapolate a beloved product characteristic** | What does the product do that users love? What is that at human scale? | Collins: "extrapolate a beloved characteristic in the product". Dropbox went "From syncing files to syncing teams" [19]. |
| **Human truth or insight** | What do customers really get, beyond the function? | Airbnb: "Belonging the world over was Airbnb's truth and its differentiator" [16]. |
| **A behavior or state** | How does the product behave? Can the identity behave the same way? | Google's dots express listening, thinking, replying, incomprehension, and confirmation: "Google's intelligence at work" [20]. |
| **A tension resolved** | Which tension from §2.3 does the brand resolve? | Mozilla's process weighed playful (Dino) against gravitas and chose the protocol route [7]. |
| **A character or agent** | Who could guide people through the category? | Coinbase's "Crypto Friendly Giant", "a lovably clumsy colossus with giant-sized helping hands" [23]. |
| **Reinterpret existing equity** | What do people already recognize? What hidden structure does it have? | Slack's octothorpe rebuilt from "a speech bubble and lozenge" [17]. Deliveroo's kangaroo simplified "to become a true brand icon" [25]. |
| **The product's own rhythm or structure** | What is the unit of the product (a message, a block, a slot)? | WhatsApp: "Modular graphic blocks mirror the rhythm of conversation" [24]. |
| **A movement or shift** | What change does the brand enable? | Uber: "movement ignites opportunity" [21]. |

**Procedure.**
1. Write 10+ raw candidates (2+ per method) as sentence + metaphor.
2. Run the tests in §4.1 and keep 3.
3. For each survivor, write the three expressions in words.
4. Present the strongest **one** as the recommendation, and the other two briefly as alternatives.
   The user approves one concept. That approval closes stage 3.
5. Store the approved concept, the two rejected ones, and the reasons in the brand core.

**Limit.** A concept is a creative device, not evidence. The cases below document how studios explained
their work. They do not show that concept-led identities perform better [E3 for any performance claim].

### 4.3 Concept language that works for a non-designer

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "The brand idea is innovation." | "The idea is a metronome: everything we make keeps the same steady beat." |
| "Our logo represents trust and growth." | "The mark is one pulse repeated. When it moves, it moves on the beat." |
| "Modern, clean, minimal." | "Quiet like a clinic waiting room should be: few elements, nothing blinking." |

**Patterns across the cases**

| Pattern | Cases | Rule for the agent |
|---|---|---|
| The concept is stated in words before it is drawn. | All | Get the sentence approved before any logo sketch. |
| The mark is a unit the system reuses. | Slack (bubble), Dropbox (diamond), Deliveroo (angles), Stack Overflow (blocks) | Ask: what piece of the mark can become icons, patterns, and layouts? |

| Equity is kept and re-explained. | Slack, Deliveroo, Mozilla (name), Windows (name) | Before inventing, check what the brand already owns (§3.2). |
| Tech brands borrow the product's own structure. | Mozilla (URL syntax), WhatsApp (conversation rhythm), Google (assistant states) | Look inside the product for the metaphor before looking outside. |

## 5. Naming basics

Naming is a separate discipline. This section covers the minimum the agent needs when the name is not fixed
(F2). **Legal clearance requires a trademark lawyer.** Online searches only screen out obvious conflicts.

### 5.1 Name types

| Igor taxonomy [29] | Definition | Examples [29] | Usual trademark strength (USPTO spectrum [30]) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Functional** | "Named after a person, purely descriptive of what the company or product does," or a functional prefix or suffix. | Infoseek, LookSmart | Often *descriptive*: registrable only in certain cases, such as after acquiring distinctiveness through long use [30]. |
| **Invented** | A made-up word, or a non-English word that is not widely known. | Agilent, Google | *Fanciful*: "invented words" that only have meaning in relation to their goods or services (Exxon, Pepsi) [30]. Strongest. |
| **Experiential** | "A direct connection to something real", literal "with a touch of imagination". | Netscape, Palm Pilot | Usually *suggestive* [30]. |
| **Evocative** | Evokes the positioning rather than the product: "evoking memories, stories, and many levels of association." | Virgin, Apple | *Arbitrary* when the word has no link to the goods (Apple for computers) [30]. Strong. |
| (Generic) | The common name of the goods. | "Bicycle" for bicycles [30] | "Generic trademarks aren't even trademarks" [30]. Never use. |

### 5.2 Screening checklist

| Check | How | Pass condition |
|---|---|---|
| Fit with strategy | Does the name support the chosen route and concept? | It can carry the concept sentence. |
| Pronounce and spell | Say it aloud to 5 people; ask them to type it. The USPTO also advises considering whether the public will remember, pronounce, and spell the mark [30]. | Most spell it right after hearing it once. |
| Meaning in target languages | Check every language in B5 with a native speaker: slang, offensive meanings, unfortunate sounds. The USPTO flags different or offensive meanings in translation [30]. | No negative meaning. |
| Category descriptiveness | Is it the generic or merely descriptive term for the category? | Fanciful, arbitrary, or suggestive [30]. |
| Domain and handles | Check the main domain, country domains for B5 markets, and handles on the platforms in F3. | Acceptable domain available or buyable; consistent handles. |
| Search engine collision | Search the name with the category word. | Not dominated by a same-category business. |
| Trademark screening | Search the registers below for identical and similar marks in the relevant classes. | No identical or close mark for related goods/services. |
| Lawyer clearance | Hand the shortlist to a trademark attorney for a full clearance search and opinion. | Written opinion before launch spend. |

### 5.3 Trademark search tools (verified 2026-10 unless marked)

| Register | Tool | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| United States (USPTO) | Trademark Search, https://tmsearch.uspto.gov/ [31] | The USPTO page also links guidance on comprehensive clearance searching [31]. |
| International (WIPO) | Global Brand Database, https://branddb.wipo.int/ [32] | Covers Madrid System international marks, Lisbon appellations, Article 6ter emblems, WHO INNs, and marks from participating national and regional offices. WIPO recommends also checking national registers directly, since they may not be fully included [32]. Supports image-similarity search [32]. |
| Multi-office (EU network) | TMview, https://www.tmdn.org/tmview/ [33] | Free, single place to search marks from participating offices: "More than 101 million trade marks" from 76 offices as of January 2022 [33]. Data is "displayed as received, without any kind of guarantee"; verify with the office before legal action [33]. Current counts are higher [unverified]. |
| European Union (EUIPO) | eSearch plus, https://euipo.europa.eu/eSearch/ [unverified: page not opened in this pass] | EU trade marks and designs. |
| Portugal (INPI) | INPI portal, https://inpi.justica.gov.pt [34] | The portal links to the trademark ("Marca") service; the exact search-tool URL was not confirmed in this pass [unverified]. Portuguese marks are also expected in TMview [unverified]. |

**Apply.** Search identical and similar spellings, sound-alikes, and translations, in the classes for
the goods and services the brand will sell (Nice classification; WIPO's Global G&S Terms Explorer helps
choose terms and classes [32]).
**Limit.** No online search proves a name is free to use. Unregistered rights, pending applications, and
similarity judgments need a lawyer. Say this to the user every time naming comes up.

Every later stage (visual territory, logo, identity system, website, social) reads this file before it
starts and cites the field it relies on. Only the brand-strategist or creative-director changes it, and every
change goes into the decisions log. Store it as `brand-core.md` (or YAML) in the project.

### 6.1 Fields

| Field | Required at | Content rule |
|---|---|---|
| `name` | Stage 1 | Name, pronunciation, trademark status. |

## 7. Mistakes and myths

| Mistake or myth | Why it's wrong | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Let's start with the logo." | The mark has nothing to express until route and concept exist. Every case in §4.4 states its concept in words first. | Run stages 1–3 and get approvals first. |
| Asking a non-designer "what's your brand personality?" | Abstract questions produce generic adjectives. | Use sliders, this-or-that pairs, and cards (§1.4). |
| Values like "innovation, integrity, excellence" | Nearly every company claims them; they cost nothing. | 3 ranked values, each with a behavior that costs something. |
| Three routes that are the same idea in different words | No real choice, so the user picks on wording or taste. | Each route gives up something different (§2.3). |
| Merging two routes to please everyone | "A boardroom compromise to please no-one" [7]. | Choose one; log the other as a road not taken. |
| "Our brand must be unique in customers' minds or we fail." | Most buyers see little difference between the brands they buy [9][10]. | Make choices with positioning; build recognition with distinctive assets (§3.1–3.2). |
| "Differentiation doesn't matter, only distinctiveness." | Overstated reading of Ehrenberg-Bass [9]; new and B2B brands need a reason to be considered. | Do both: a clear position and ownable assets. |
| "Consistency increases revenue 23%." | Survey of marketers' attributions; original not verifiable [27]. Grade E3. | Argue for consistency through distinctive-asset memory (§3.5). |
| "Our archetype is the Hero, so research says…" | No validation found; archetypes are a vocabulary [E3]. | Use for tone only; never as evidence. |
| Using category codes (shield for security, brain or sparkle for AI, globe for global) | Shared codes fall in the "Avoid" quadrant: famous but not unique [11]. | Derive the metaphor from the brand's own product or world (§4.2). |
| Trusting the team's sense of what customers recognize | Marketers overestimate fame and underestimate uniqueness [13]. | Test assets with category buyers. |
| Throwing away recognized equity in a rebrand | Use-or-Lose assets are hard to rebuild [11]. | Keep and re-explain (Slack, Deliveroo). |
| "The name is free; I checked the domain." | Domains say nothing about trademark rights. | Screen the registers (§5.3), then a lawyer. |
| A concept that only produces a logo | The identity system and motion will have nothing to draw from. | Require three expressions (§4.1). |
| A concept the product can't back up | Brand promises the product breaks damage trust. | Truthful test (§4.1); proof in `unique_attributes`. |
| Quoting a framework as science | Positioning templates and brand sprints are practice [E2], not experiments. | Say "a common studio method", not "research shows". |
